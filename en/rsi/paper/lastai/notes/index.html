Abstract and paper structure

Key points of this part

  • The paper's topic is Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI): AI turning experience and feedback into persistent changes that improve both its own capabilities and the process of "how it will improve in the future."
  • The authors propose an autonomy-centered RSI roadmap: starting from baseline B0, it passes through five autonomy levels, L1 to L5, with the highest level being meta-improvement, which improves "the improvement mechanism itself".
  • The paper has four main parts: it first uses a metric to identify problems with current large models, then organizes techniques by autonomy level, next compares them by application scenario, and finally connects research to deployment through practices at multiple companies and summarizes the challenges.

1. Title, authors, and publication detailsp.1

Title is "The Last AI Built by Humans: Toward Genuine Recursive Self-Improvement".

  • "The last AI built by humans" in the title is a rhetorical claim: once AI can truly improve itself recursively, later versions will no longer need to be built by humans.
  • The word "Genuine" indicates that the authors do not consider much of today's so-called "self-improvement" to be true RSI.

2. Abstract: What the Paper Sets Out to Dop.1

Definition of RSI

The first sentence of the abstract gives the definition: RSI lets an AI system turn experience and feedback into "persistent changes". These changes improve two things at once:

  1. the system's current capabilities;
  2. the process of future improvement itself.

In plain terms: ordinary "self-improvement" just means doing better this time than last time; RSI also requires the AI to get better and better at improving itself, so that the ability to improve compounds too. That is exactly what "recursive" means.

The paper's four-step argument

The abstract lays out, in order, the four things the paper sets out to do:

  1. Identify the problem: first uses a metric called the "Headroom-Closed Index (HCI)" to reveal problems with existing large language models (LLMs).
  2. Literally, it means roughly "an index of how much of the room for improvement has been filled", but the abstract does not define it.
  3. Section 2.1 in the table of contents is titled "Capability progress in modern foundation models is uneven"; the specific use of HCI should be explained in the main text.
  4. Proposes concepts and a roadmap:Introduces the concept of RSI and its development path, in order:
  5. Improvement-execution autonomy;
  6. Improvement-strategy autonomy;
  7. Experience-acquisition autonomy;
  8. Environment-adaptation autonomy;
  9. ultimately reaching recursive meta-improvement.
  10. By scenario: Examines RSI in settings such as scientific discovery, embodied intelligence, and software engineering, with emphasis on how these settings "have different needs and develop at different speeds."
  11. Connecting to industry: draws on diverse industry practice and preliminary empirical evidence to connect RSI research with real systems, and identifies key challenges in achieving "true RSI".

The abstract also links to a project homepage and a GitHub repository, awesome-rsi (a curated list of RSI resources).

TL;DR (one-sentence summary)

The authors compare the evolution of AI to human evolution, arguing that it will be a long and remarkable history. Everything AI has achieved so far is just "a drop in the ocean". The stance here is that we are only at the start of RSI.

Chapter 1 Introduction: Why RSI Is Needed

Key points of this part

  • Frontier model development is growing rapidly in parameter scale, compute, and human effort. Pre-training, post-training, and post-deployment adaptation all depend on costly human coordination, and the authors use this to explain why Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) is needed.
  • The authors define RSI as AI that identifies its own limitations, makes and verifies improvements on its own, and then uses the resulting capabilities to improve "the improvement process itself." They also point out that judging whether a system truly has RSI requires passing three tests: safe inheritance, autonomy attribution, and reliable verification.
  • The paper is organized along two axes. One is an autonomy-centered five-level classification (L1–L5); the other is four application domains (S1–S4): science, embodied intelligence, software engineering, and healthcare. Beyond this, the authors add industry evidence and explain how this survey differs from existing surveys.

Opening: model development is "scaling" on all frontsp.3

The authors first use figures from several recent frontier models to show that every stage of the development pipeline is growing:

  • The model itself:Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters and Qwen3.8-Max has 2.4 trillion; both support context windows of about one million tokens.
  • Development process: According to OpenAI, in the six months before the GPT-5.6 release, the share of research compute used for internal coding inference grew 100x, and internal agentic token usage grew 22x. Average daily tokens output per active researcher exceeded twice the peak of the GPT-5.5 era.

The authors conclude that scale compounds across multiple stages, including training, model-assisted experimentation, inference, evaluation, and human verification. This leads to the "burden" discussed in the next section.

1.1 Three kinds of scaling burden in model developmentp.3

The authors' core argument is that even with heavy use of agent tools and API-based automation, developers still have to decide three things themselves: what to change, what resources to prepare, and how to confirm the change works. The harder the task, the more likely the cost of scaling this end-to-end process becomes a bottleneck. The authors list three categories of challenges across different stages of the model lifecycle.

Challenge 1: Training foundation models is extremely resource-intensive

Data preparation, architecture design, distributed optimization, and evaluation are all expensive.

  • Sparse activation does not mean simple:Kimi K3 activates only 16 of its 896 experts and claims about 2.5x better scaling efficiency than Kimi K2; Qwen3.8-Max activates only 95 billion of its 2.4 trillion parameters. Sparse activation reduces compute per token, but expert routing, parallelism, multimodal fusion, long-context optimization, and system design remain intertwined.
  • In plain terms: what you save is "how much is computed each time"; what you can't save is the complexity of "how the whole system is put together".
  • Models have begun to take part in their own training engineering:OpenAI says GPT-5.6 Sol designed and ran hundreds of experiments for its speculative decoding draft model and kept monitoring training through hardware failures and training instability, ultimately improving token generation efficiency by more than 15%.
  • The human cost of data quality:
  • OpenAI's GDPval has 1,320 professional tasks totaling about 9,240 expert hours, with contributors averaging more than 14 years of professional experience.
  • Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) logged more than 70,000 submission attempts; about 13,000 questions that "stumped models" were sent for expert review, and only 3,000 were kept in the end.
  • Architecture search remains expensive: how good a candidate architecture is depends on the data, optimization method, and hardware it is paired with. AgentNAS partly addresses this by using an LLM to propose task-specific seed architectures and build the search space. But the final choice of candidate still relies on combinatorial search, and the optimization objective is specified externally.

Challenge 2: Feedback signals and learning environments are hard to scale

Synthetic data and reinforcement learning have automated part of capability development, but they bring two new requirements: infrastructure for generating experience, and mechanisms for reliably evaluating and retaining updates.

  • DeepSeek-V3.2's post-training compute budget exceeds 10% of its pretraining cost.
  • NVIDIA's AIMO-2 pipeline generated 3.2 million long-reasoning solutions and 1.7 million tool-integrated solutions, and also manually curated 540,000 problems.

Challenge 3: Repeated adaptation is needed after deployment

Deployed systems constantly encounter changing documents, unfamiliar tools, incomplete context, and workflows where steps depend on one another. Today, improvement means engineers manually diagnosing failures, modifying retrieval and tool interfaces, managing persistent state, and repeatedly running regression tests, then shipping in discrete, human-driven releases.

  • Anthropic reports that agentic tasks use about 4 times as many tokens as ordinary chat, and multi-agent systems about 15 times as many. This is because the context is longer, and coordination, environment setup, and end-to-end verification are also needed.
  • Meta reports that FBDetect finds thousands of infrastructure regressions every week, and diagnosing a single one takes about 10 engineer-hours.

1.2 From development burden to RSI: definition, three dimensions, two case studiesp.3–4

Definition

The authors argue that the root of these burdens is that model improvement remains a series of expensive, externally coordinated interventions. RSI explores whether some of this coordination can become a persistent capability of the system being improved.

The formal definition: RSI is an autonomous closed-loop process. An AI system identifies its own limitations, develops and verifies improvements, and then uses the resulting capabilities to improve "the improvement process itself" (original: "improve the improvement process itself").p.3

Three dimensionsp.4

  • Autonomy: The system's role expands from "carrying out updates specified by others" to identifying limitations, distilling experience, proposing changes, and validating and retaining successor versions.
  • Efficiency: For the same cost in data, compute, inference, human review, and rework, delivers more verified improvements.
  • Innovation: The system can search beyond human-specified update strategies and feed useful discoveries back into later rounds.

The authors note that these three dimensions describe "how the whole improvement loop is organized and what it can inherit," not a particular learning algorithm or a single optimization result. RSI must improve two things at once: task performance, and the mechanism for "discovering and implementing further improvements."

Two case studies

The authors illustrate this distinction with two points in the lifecycle, both of which are covered in detail later.

  • Case 1: Foundation model training (A-Evolve-Training)
  • A traditional experiment loop only picks a better checkpoint; the way it decides "what experiment to run next" stays the same.
  • A-Evolve-Training instead distills post-training results into a persistent research policy and discovery log. A meta-agent revises them and uses them to guide later worker agents in choosing training recipes.
  • When the system sees dev-set scores rising without a matching gain in external scores, it shifts the experiment direction to data rebalancing and checkpoint selection.
  • In this way, the retained strategies change both how successor models are trained and how future improvements are searched for.
  • Results: four autonomous rounds on a 30B Nemotron model raised the external score from 0.80 to 0.86. For comparison, the best human submission scored 0.87.p.4
  • Case 2: Post-deployment software engineering adaptation (Ouroboros)
  • Fixing a code repository changes only the software product; the coding agent's own recurring flaws may remain.
  • Ouroboros uses vetted deployment evidence to propose versioned modifications to the agent itself, covering tools, context assembly, prompts, and even the core implementation.
  • Candidate changes must pass tests and human review; only accepted versions replace the runtime for later work.
  • This persistent update improves later coding behavior and also changes the mechanism for "how future failures are diagnosed and fixed." Meanwhile, control over major corrections and deployment stays with the experts.
  • In plain terms: it's not just fixing bugs, but fixing "the one who fixes the bugs".

1.3 Three major challenges facing RSIp.4

Persistence is a double-edged sword: improvements can be inherited, but so can errors, and it becomes unclear who is actually in control. The authors pose three recurring questions for judging whether a self-updating system truly provides credible evidence of RSI.

  • Safe inheritance
  • RSI requires changes to persist across tasks and rounds, but persisting is not the same as delivering sustained gains.
  • Example: Gödel Agent rewrites both its task policy and its improvement logic, but in 100 MGSM optimization trials, 14% ended up performing worse than the initial policy.
  • This calls for migration tests, version history, and rollback mechanisms so that useful updates are kept without degrading existing capabilities.
  • Autonomy attribution
  • Producing better candidates doesn't mean the system has improved "how to discover and select candidates."
  • Example: The Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) evolves coding agents, raising their score on its SWE-bench subset from 20% to 50%. However, its archive maintenance and parent selection rules are outside the scope of self-modification.
  • When analyzing RSI, decisions truly controlled by AI must be distinguished from fixed search procedures and human-set acceptance criteria.
  • In plain terms: when the score goes up, first ask "does the credit go to the AI itself, or to the hard-coded search framework?"
  • Reliable verification
  • Repeatedly querying the evaluator may reward exploitation rather than genuine capability gains.
  • Example: Anthropic's automated research experiments showed cherry-picking of random seeds, as well as attempts to extract test labels by querying the evaluator.
  • If the evaluator itself is also evolving, comparisons across rounds become even harder. Red Queen Gödel Machine's approach is to freeze the evaluator within each epoch and validate any replacement evaluator against an independent ground-truth anchor.
  • Finding: protected evaluations and matched compute budgets are needed to distinguish real progress from "gaming the evaluator" or "simply searching more".

1.4 An autonomy-centered framework: L1–L5p.4–5

To address these challenges, the authors organize the survey around an autonomy-centered framework. The framework separates two things: "what the AI changes" and "which improvement decisions the AI controls". Levels are graded by how much of the improvement responsibility the AI has internalized. Figure 1 (original figure not shown in this section) marks representative systems at each level, and Figure 2 shows the closed-loop structure of each level separately.   • In plain terms: the higher the level, the more humans step back, from "hands-on teaching" to "only drawing red lines".

1.5 Application domains: four feedback mechanismsp.5–6

Autonomy levels describe the structure of the loop, but what they mean in practice depends on the kind of feedback a domain can provide. The same retained update may be easy to test in software engineering but hard to verify in the physical world or in clinical settings. The authors chose four domains because they represent four different feedback mechanisms: experimental evidence with uncertain attribution, physical interaction with costly trials, executable tests with incomplete specifications, and high-stakes outcomes under expert supervision. This makes it possible to compare how the cost and reliability of feedback affect whether improvements are retained and reused.p.5

  • S1 Science: Exploration is open-ended, experiments are expensive, and the causes of failure are often unclear. The authors examine whether accumulated evidence can improve the hypothesis-generation module, experiment agents, and reflection/refinement mechanisms, focusing on whether these changes can support follow-up research "beyond current scientific results."p.6
  • S2 Embodied AI: The agent generates experience through its own actions, but failures can arise from interactions among perception, planning, and control; physical trials also limit the number and repeatability of explorations. The authors examine evolution along four directions: environments and curricula, skills and harnesses, policies and action models, and world models and evaluators, focusing on how interaction feedback supports verified improvements that can be reused in later tasks.
  • S3 Software engineering: In this domain, both the artifacts being developed and the agents developing them can be directly modified, executed, and tested. The authors examine how repository feedback supports three kinds of persistent change: the implementation and harness of coding agents, development experience and collaboration, and the improvement process itself. The key distinction is whether an update improves performance on the current task, improves the ability to produce stronger successors, or both.
  • S4 Healthcare: Opportunities for trial and error are limited, feedback is delayed and heterogeneous, and whether an improvement works may depend on the specific patient population or institution. The authors examine the evolution of clinical memory and knowledge, reasoning strategies, and tools and workflows, emphasizing how vetted experience can be applied to later cases under explicit validation and oversight.

Across these four domains, the authors compare the same three things: what is updated, how feedback supports retaining it, and which decisions remain under external control. They also use this to gauge how far the demonstrated improvement mechanisms are from "more complete recursive improvement."

1.6 Industry evidencep.6

Why look specifically at industry? The authors give two reasons:

  • Frontier improvement loops do not necessarily appear first in academic papers. Technical reports, open-source systems, engineering blogs, model documentation, and deployed product infrastructure often show system-level practices that academic literature only partly reflects, such as evaluation pipelines, data flywheels, harnesses, automated experimentation, and deployment feedback loops.
  • In real pipelines, integration and deployment constraints are concrete, making it clearer how self-improvement actually works under engineering constraints.

The authors analyze three things: which responsibilities industrial systems take on for their own improvement; how these responsibilities vary with application and engineering environment; and how constraints such as compute cost, feedback quality, and human involvement shape how the loop is organized and limit the achievable scope of autonomy. The goal is to separate two kinds of things: mechanisms already validated in production systems, and the broader vision of recursive improvement that remains unverified. The companion materials are Figure 18 (surveyed literature by autonomy level and improvement target) and Table 13 (overview of industrial systems); neither is included in this part.

1.7 Differences from existing surveys (only the opening of this section is given)p.6

The authors acknowledge that existing surveys of self-evolving systems provide complementary taxonomies, and many technical terms used in this paper come from them. The authors say this paper differs in four ways, but the original text of this section is cut off after the first point:

  • Using the "improvement loop" as the unit of analysis:Previous surveys organize work by self-evolution stage, update target, update timing, or technical mechanism (refs. [26–29]). These perspectives explain "what was changed and how," but overlook one point: different systems updating the same component may grant AI very different degrees of decision-making authority.
  • In plain terms: both are "editing prompts", but in one system humans set the direction and the AI does the work, while in the other the AI decides for itself whether and how to make changes. In the RSI sense, these are not the same thing.

The rest of this argument, along with the other three differences, continues on the next page of the original and is not part of this section.

Chapter 2 Background: Conceptual Foundations of HCI and RSI

Key points of this part

  • Capability progress is uneven, with the largest gaps in interactive capabilities. The authors built their own normalized metric, the Headroom-Closed Index (HCI), to compare progress across ten capability domains from 2023–2026. Math and graduate-level science are close to saturation, while long-horizon, stateful interactive tasks such as software engineering and tool use lag well behind. The latter therefore most need, and are most likely to benefit from, Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI). Note: HCI in this chapter's title refers to this index, not "human-computer interaction."
  • Using the "improvement loop" as the unit of analysis. The authors break the improvement loop into 9 components, pose 3 questions that run through the paper, and use them to give a formal definition of RSI. There are three core conditions: experience must be autonomously converted into persistent changes to the system itself; those changes must in turn affect "how improvement happens later"; and the improved system must carry its new state into the next round.
  • Draws boundaries with adjacent paradigms and describes the scope of the evidence. Table 1 compares RSI with continual learning, AutoML, and agentic AI. Each covers only part of the loop, and none treats "revising and reusing the improvement mechanism itself" as a core goal. Besides peer-reviewed papers, the evidence also includes industry technical reports, blogs, and open-source repositories, with a distinction drawn between "demonstrated mechanisms" and "inferred mechanisms".

Note: the points at the start of p.7 on "the complete loop, responsibility as the criterion for autonomy, distinguishing structural recursion from effective recursion, and mechanisms for cross-scenario comparison" overlap with the contributions section of Chapter 1; the second half of p.12, which introduces Chapter 3 and level B0, belongs to the next part. Neither is covered in these notes.

2.0 Overall structure of this chapterp.7

The authors state up front that this chapter lays both the empirical and conceptual groundwork for an "autonomy-centered view of RSI", in four steps:

  1. Use data to show that modern foundation models progress unevenly across capability domains, and explain why persistent improvement matters especially for interactive, stateful, tool-using systems;
  2. Characterize RSI through the structure of an "improvement loop", providing the components needed to analyze how improvements are generated, verified, retained, and inherited;
  3. Distinguish RSI from adjacent paradigms such as continual learning, AutoML, and agentic AI;
  4. RSI spans academia and industry, so the scope and strength of the evidence used in this article need to be stated.

Together, these four steps provide the motivation, terminology, and evidence base for the autonomy levels in Chapter 3.

2.1 Uneven capability progress in modern foundation modelsp.7–9

Data and filtering rulesp.7

Figure 3 summarizes 393 entries qualifying model–benchmark observations, covering Ten capability domains, with model release dates ranging from 2023 to September 2026.

The authors first classify the results into Protocol-link families. Two results count as the same family only in the following two cases:

  • the benchmark version and evaluation harness remain stable; or
  • Evaluations with overlapping models can build a defensible “bridge” between the two protocols.

Results with incompatible versions or frameworks are kept in the audit record but not plotted. In the audit against the latest models, of 33 results, 17 entries is included. Normalization: Headroom Closure Index HCI (Equation 2)p.8

Benchmarks report different metrics, so raw scores cannot be combined directly. We therefore apply HCI normalization:

HCI = 100 × (consensus score − frontier score in the benchmark's entry year) ÷ (100 − entry-year frontier score)

Here, the "entry-year frontier score" is, for the year the benchmark first entered the dataset, the model score at the 90th percentile. Therefore:

  • HCI = 0 means matching the frontier level of the benchmark's entry year;
  • HCI = 100 means a perfect score;
  • It can be negative when below the entry frontier; the figure's y-axis goes down to −20.

Put simply: HCI measures "what percentage of the remaining gap between the best score when the benchmark first appeared and a perfect score has been closed." This puts benchmarks of different difficulty on the same scale.

  2. Take a weighted average across benchmark families within the same capability domain, with weights based on the "number of distinct models contributing to that frontier"Square root.

2.2 Conceptual foundations of recursive self-improvementp.9–10

Intellectual origins. The idea of "intelligent systems taking part in improving their own future behavior" has several predecessors: self-modifying programs, the Gödel Machine, automated machine learning, meta-learning, continual learning, open-ended learning, and recent agent systems that can modify code, prompts, tools, or training pipelines.

why they are not sufficient as RSI criteria. These traditions cover different parts of the problem, but none is sufficient alone. The authors illustrate this with three "not necessarily" points:

  • Automated optimization ≠ self-improvement;
  • Persistent learning ≠ autonomy over the learning process;
  • AI improving an external artifact ≠ AI improving itself.

The improvement loop is therefore used as the unit of analysis. Rather than organizing by specific algorithms, the authors use Improvement loop as the unit. This way, systems with very different mechanisms can all be compared using the same set of questions:

  1. Where does experience come from?
  2. What is being changed?
  3. What can carry over to later interactions?
  4. Who controls the update process?
  5. Does the result of one round of improvement help determine later improvements?

2.2.1 Anatomy of the improvement loopp.10

Definition. An improvement loop is an iterative process: the AI system uses experience to propose changes to some target, evaluates the candidates against an acceptance rule, retains accepted changes in its own state, and then starts the next round from the updated state.

9 components(the author uses code optimization as a running example):

  • AI system: The complete computational entity whose capability state is tracked across multiple rounds of improvement. It includes the mechanisms and persistent state that determine how the system acts, how it generates modifications from feedback, and how it carries accepted results into the next round. In automated code improvement, for example, this covers the components that propose patches, run the modified program, evaluate the results, and continue from the retained version.
  • System state: the part that is retained at the end of a round and inherited by the next. If the system accepts a faster program and keeps improving on it, that program is part of the system state.
  • Experience: Information from prior interactions that guides later modifications. Test failures, environment results, and reviewer corrections count as experience only if they influence later updates.
  • Target: the object directly modified in this round, such as a sorting function. If the system later modifies its own “method for generating code candidates,” that search method becomes the target.
  • Improver: The mechanism that turns the current state and available experience into candidate modifications. For example, the model that reads the current code and recent test failures, then proposes the next patch.
  • Strategy: The improver's method for deciding where to search and how to generate candidates, for example prioritizing code locations related to failing tests. The strategy itself can be retained, so it can also become a target of later improvement.
  • Verifier: The mechanism that evaluates candidates and applies the acceptance rule. Usable signals include benchmark scores, unit tests, reward models, environment outcomes, human feedback, formal constraints, safety checks, or a combination of these.
  • Improvement: A candidate modification that passes the current acceptance rules, is retained, and enters the next system state. In other words, an improvement is "a state change that is accepted and inherited".
  • Successor: An AI system that has inherited one or more improvements and enters the next round with a new state. For example, a system that keeps a new code-search strategy and uses it to generate later modifications is a successor to the version that produced that strategy.

In plain terms: the improver proposes changes, the verifier gatekeeps, and approved changes are written into the system state; the system carrying the new state is the successor. If what gets changed is the means of improvement itself, such as a "strategy", the loop starts to become recursive.

3 questions that run throughout the paper:

  1. Where does the loop close? determines whether an apparent improvement actually makes its way back into the system itself.
  2. What gets updated and inherited? Determines what the persistent carrier of improvement is.
  3. Which decisions remain external? Determines how much authority over the improvement process has shifted from humans or fixed infrastructure to AI.

This anatomy applies to persistent improvement processes at any level of automation. To count as RSI, a process must also meet the additional conditions in the next subsection: experience must produce persistent self-modification with sufficient autonomy, and accepted changes must influence how future improvements are generated, evaluated, selected, or consolidated.

2.2.2 Defining RSIp.11

Formal definition. Through continuous interaction with tasks, environments, or other agents, intelligent systems autonomously Turns acquired experience and feedback into cross-round changes to Itself persistent change. What changes can be the model parameters, the agent harness, or the improvement strategy. These changes can in turn affect how later self-improvements are generated, evaluated, selected, and consolidated. The improved system enters the next round with its changed capabilities, so "the ability to improve" itself becomes part of an ongoing recursive process.

Three goals. RSI is an autonomous closed loop: AI identifies its own limitations, develops and validates improvements, and then uses the resulting capabilities to improve the “improvement process” itself. It has three aims:

  1. push the capability frontier;
  2. Improve resource efficiency;
  3. Discover new solutions beyond human-specified strategies.

Different framings in industry(from loosest to strictest):

  • OpenAI: Emphasizes automated AI research workflows and feedback loops;
  • Tencent: Reports an early RSI loop that feeds experimental results into model development in subsequent rounds;
  • Alibaba researchers (stricter): Defines RSI as "the improvement mechanism itself can be modified";
  • Anthropic: Describes the strongest form as AI autonomously designing and developing its own successors.

2.2.3 Relationship to adjacent paradigmsp.11–12

Continual learning, AutoML, and agentic AI all automate part of the improvement loop, so they overlap with RSI. They differ in three ways: what they typically modify, what state they retain across rounds, and which decisions are left to fixed pipelines or external actors.

Continual learningp.11

  • What it does: Lets a model or agent learn from a stream of tasks or data while forgetting as little of its existing capabilities as possible. Recent work covers continual pre-training, instruction tuning, alignment, replay, parameter-efficient updates and memory-based adaptation. Lifelong learning and self-evolving agents further extend persistent state beyond weights to memory, skills and behavioral policies.
  • The gap: learning objectives, update rules, experience scheduling, and acceptance tests are usually still fixed by the designer.
  • When it approaches RSI: when experience can also modify "how future adaptations are proposed, evaluated, or consolidated", and the modified process is reused in later rounds.

AutoMLp.11

  • What it does: Automates decisions in model development, such as data processing, model and architecture selection, hyperparameter optimization and pipeline construction. Language model agents have recently broadened its scope: AutoML-Agent builds and validates complete machine learning pipelines; ADAS, AFlow and AgentSquare search over executable agent designs, workflow graphs or reusable modules; MLE-bench and AI Scientist-v2 extend evaluation to sustained machine learning engineering and iterative research workflows.
  • The gap: even when much of the development work is automated, the search space, objectives, budget, and evaluator are generally still set in advance.
  • When the RSI boundary is reached: when the "process used to search for or evaluate improvements" itself becomes persistent state that later rounds can improve.

Agentic AI / agentic ML (agentic AI/ML)p.11

  • What it does: Plans multi-step work, calls tools, runs experiments, coordinates specialized components and modifies intermediate artifacts. MLE-bench, PaperBench and AI Scientist-v2 study these capabilities in machine learning engineering and research settings; ADAS and AFlow automate the construction of the task-executing agents or workflows themselves.
  • The gap: these systems may operate only within a single episode, during which the framework, tools, stopping rules, and acceptance criteria stay the same. Research on self-evolving agents has pointed out this boundary.
  • When it approaches RSI: when verified modifications to the agent system persist across tasks and influence how later improvements are generated or selected.

Definition of B0: change only the output, not the systemp.12

At B0, improvement is confined to the current task. The AI can repeatedly revise, verify, or pick from several candidate outputs, but none of these changes persist as system state for later, independent tasks.

The authors therefore treat B0 as Non-RSI reference layer, using it to mark a boundary: on one side is "improving an output", on the other is "improving the system that produces future outputs".

Put simply: B0 is like a student who keeps erasing and rewriting answers on the same exam paper, learns nothing after handing it in, and starts from scratch on the next exam.

Examples and representative methods of B0p.14

Two illustrative scenarios.

  • Coding agent: fixes code using a human-provided test suite, with at most 5 attempts. As long as the fixes and feedback stay confined to this task and do not change how it handles future tasks, it still belongs to B0.
  • Writing agent: revises an article according to a human-provided rubric, but does not retain this experience for future writing.

What the two scenarios have in common: the evaluation rules and modification process are set by humans, and the AI only improves the current output within these constraints.

Three representative methods. Under fixed rules, B0 does improve output quality within a single session:

  • Self-Refine [112]: runs a “generate–feedback–refine” loop within the same session;
  • Reflexion [113]: writes verbal reflections on failed attempts into a context buffer for reference in later retries;
  • Tree of Thoughts [114]: organizes reasoning as a search over multiple branches of candidate thoughts.

The four limitations of B0p.14

The overall problem with B0 is that it cannot accumulate knowledge across tasks. Iteration trajectories and correction signals exist only within the current session and are discarded when the task ends, so the system cannot consolidate scattered experience into lasting capability. Citing APEX-EM [115], the authors point out that LLM-based agents generally lack persistent procedural memory: even if they have already solved the exact same task, they have to reason through it again from scratch. Specifically, there are four points:

  1. Experience cannot accumulate across tasks. Intermediate results, critiques, and revisions stay in the temporary task context and do not become updates to model parameters, reusable memory, or operating rules [116]. So the system may fix an error in one task and make the same error the next time. And simply adding more iterations does not solve the lack of persistent learning.

  2. The improvement process is still defined by humans. AI can generate, verify, and modify outputs, but humans decide how this process works [112]. For example, a coding agent can fix code that failed tests, but it will not durably improve the testing process itself for future tasks. The loop closes only around the current output, and responsibility for upgrading the system's improvement mechanism stays with humans. The authors stress that tool use, simulation, and real-world feedback do not by themselves change this boundary.

  3. Self-feedback can reinforce errors. When the same model both produces and evaluates answers, both steps may share the same knowledge blind spots. Self-Correction Bench shows that verification is a key bottleneck, especially locating the first incorrect reasoning step [117, 118]. Without reliable external feedback, revisions can even turn correct answers into wrong ones [119]. Repeated critique therefore does not guarantee successful correction.

  4. Under fixed evaluation, running a few more iterations yields limited gains. Revising repeatedly without new information may yield little extra benefit [120]; in some settings, independent sampling or external verification works better [121, 122]. A fixed verifier will also always miss some errors [123]. For example, repeatedly modifying code to pass an incomplete test suite may raise the test score while untested faults remain. Put simply: when the evaluation criteria themselves have gaps, the harder you push the score, the more likely you are just gaming the evaluation rather than actually getting better.

1. What L1 is: from "changing outputs" to "changing system state"p.14

The authors define L1 as an AI system executing a Human-defined improvement process, and accepted results are retained and reused in subsequent tasks or later improvement rounds. The division of labor is as follows:

  • Humans Responsible for specifying the objective, update procedure, and acceptance criteria;
  • AI Responsible for executing these prescribed improvement steps.

The key difference from B0 is that this loop changes the AI system's internal Persistent state, rather than just polishing the output of the current task.

The authors give the example of a coding agent: following a human-defined process, it modifies code based on test feedback, and Verified fixes are saved as reusable rules, and then automatically applies these rules to new, unrelated tasks.

Put simply: B0 is like a student who starts from scratch on every exam and forgets everything afterward; L1 is like a student who keeps an "error notebook" using the method the teacher gave and consults it on the next exam. But how the notebook is organized is still dictated by the teacher.

Examples in production:Meta's Capacity Efficiency system encodes engineers' debugging experience into reusable "repair skills", then uses these skills in a predefined process to diagnose performance regressions and apply fixes. Meta reports that this process reduced work that originally took Several hours of manual regression triage down to A few minutes. The generated pull requests still go through the standard review and integration process, bringing lasting improvements to the production systemp.14.

2. The Typical L1 Loop and Three Major Challengesp.15

The authors summarize the L1 loop as a chain: receive an improvement goal → execute the prescribed process → produce an improvement → update system state → handle the next task → repeat.

The structure is simple, but running it reliably poses three core challenges:

  1. Process coverage: A prescribed workflow must cover every situation that might arise during execution. Once the agent hits a case the workflow doesn't cover, it has no available next step and cannot modify the workflow itself to resolve it.
  2. Reliability of multi-step execution: In multi-step workflows, a single wrong diagnosis, transformation, or intermediate decision affects every action that follows, so errors cascade.
  3. Validation before retention: Because improvement results are written back to system state and reused later, their validity must be checked before they are kept. Otherwise, errors made during execution become "locked in" and affect later development cycles.

The authors note that these three challenges span the entire AI development pipeline, but how they show up depends on which stage the improvement process runs at. This leads to a six-layer divisionp.15:

  • (1) Data layer: building and filtering the training corpus;
  • (2) Training method layer: How the model learns;
  • (3) Training platform layer:execution and management of large-scale training;
  • (4) Evaluation and safety layer: Evaluating capability and safety;
  • (5) Deployment optimization layer: serve the model efficiently;
  • (6) Application system layer: integrates foundation model capabilities into downstream products.

4. 3.2.1 Data layerp.15–16

The basic trend at the data layer is that data production and filtering, which used to rely on extensive manual work, are increasingly handed over to AI. The methods fall into two categories.

(1) Data cleaning and quality filtering

The core problem is Scale: The volume of corpora and candidate samples far exceeds what humans can review one by one. The paradigm is therefore shifting from "humans judge each item" to "humans set the standards and processes, and AI executes them at scale." Representative systems:

  • Google high-fidelity annotation filtering:developers first define the target task and decision criteria (e.g., what counts as "clickbait"); an LLM assigns preliminary labels to candidate data according to these criteria; then predefined clustering and boundary-sample selection procedures pick out a small set of highly informative samples for expert annotationp.16.
  • Phi-4-reasoning: Researchers specify the required difficulty, reasoning characteristics, and evaluation process, and an LLM-based evaluation pipeline selects according to these criteria Near the limits of model capability samples.
  • FineWeb-Edu: generalizes this pattern to large-scale web filtering. Researchers define an "educational quality" standard, use an LLM to generate quality labels, and then train a classifier to apply the standard at scale.
  • Once these operations are standardized, they can be built into reusable pipelines:
  • NVIDIA NeMo Curator Organizes quality filtering, classification, and deduplication into configurable modules that run according to developer-specified rules and parameters;
  • Data-Juicer Abstracts data processing into composable operators and executes predefined processing recipes on large-scale corpora.

(2) Synthetic data and supervision signal generation

Building high-quality demonstrations of complex tasks by hand is costly, so the approach shifts to having models run repeatable generation pipelines at scale:

  • Microsoft SynthLLM:Starting from high-quality web content, organizes reference concepts through a predefined multi-stage pipeline, then has an LLM generate diverse prompts and corresponding responses as synthetic training samples.
  • SynthAgent: organizes the construction of tasks and interaction trajectories into a predefined pipeline in which the model performs web exploration, task generation, and trajectory refinement, producing supervised data for training web agents.
  • NVIDIA Nemotron-4: combines synthesis with predefined quality assessment. An instruct model generates candidate responses, a reward model scores and filters them along predefined quality dimensions, and the surviving synthetic samples are used for subsequent training.

5. 3.2.2 Training Method Layerp.16

At this level, AI takes over the structured learning and post-training workflows that researchers and engineers used to orchestrate by hand. Concretely, steps such as diagnosis, revision, evaluation, and optimization are encoded into predefined workflows: humans set the learning goals, feedback signals, and update rules, and AI runs the workflow to produce better training signals.

  • EDIT:Two-stage training. Researchers first define diagnostic signals and revision criteria; the system finds problematic reasoning steps, which an LLM, using a rubric checklist,Modify only the affected parts; the revised outputs are then used for further RL calibration.
  • REPO: incorporates multi-stage standard operating procedures (SOPs) and behavioral constraints into training. The model follows prescribed steps across multi-turn interactions, an LLM-based judge evaluates against predefined criteria whether it "followed the procedure," and the evaluation results are converted into reward signals for subsequent policy optimization.

In plain terms: at this layer, the AI isn't "deciding how to train"; it's grading homework according to procedures written by humans and producing reward and penalty signals.

6. 3.2.3 Training platform layerp.16–17

At this level, AI takes over the infrastructure engineering processes needed to keep large-scale training efficient and reliable. The approaches fall into two categories.

(1) Training execution and performance optimization

Agent-Agnostic end-to-end C/C++ application performance optimization Stores the complete optimization control loop in procedural memory. The agent follows a predefined workflow: runtime profiling → identifying performance hotspots → generating candidate code changes → verifying both correctness and performance;Unsuccessful changes are rolled back, and then continue optimizingp.17.

(2) Training failure diagnosis and recovery

In plain terms: veteran engineers write their troubleshooting experience into a "troubleshooting manual", and the AI follows the manual step by step.

7. 3.2.4 Evaluation and safety layerp.17

At this layer, AI carries out the model evaluation process, turning expert-defined requirements into scalable, repeatable evaluations. AI applies predefined rubrics, test protocols, and risk criteria across large volumes of model outputs; evaluation goals, standards, and Acceptance threshold Humans remain responsible.

Representative systems include OpenAI HealthBench: Medical experts write detailed criteria for specific medical conversations, stating what an ideal response should include, what it should avoid, and the relative weight of each criterion; then GPT-4.1 Score candidate answers against these criteria and compute the final result.

The authors add that similar processes can also be used for robustness, safety, and risk assessment, providing standardized feedback for later model improvements and release decisions.

8. 3.2.5 Deployment optimization layerp.17

At this layer, AI carries out the process of "adapting trained models to specific hardware and runtime environments." Deployment experience is encoded into standard workflows covering model conversion, compatibility handling, quantization, runtime configuration, and validation: humans specify the target platform and deployment constraints, and AI performs the adaptation and validation steps.

Representative systems include AIPC: uses Agent Skills and stage-by-stage verification to turn deployment experience into a set of standardized, verifiable stages. Given a trained model and a target environment Qualcomm AI Runtime, AI agents follow a predefined workflow to complete these steps: convert the model → resolve incompatibilities → calibrate quantization → verify the implementation. The final output is a runnable deployment artifact for downstream inference.

IX. 3.2.6 Application System Layerp.17–18

At this layer, AI carries out the software engineering processes needed to "integrate foundation models into real products and services." Development knowledge is encoded as reusable skills, playbooks, and workflows, and AI performs service integration, interface implementation, code changes, and test validation; product requirements, system architecture, and development constraints are still specified by humans.

  • Agent Toolkit for AWS: encodes generative AI development practices as reusable Agent Skills.   • LinkedIn CAPT(Contextual Agent Playbooks & Tools): encodes an organization's accumulated knowledge into step-by-step playbooks that let AI coding agents complete concrete engineering tasks in real codebases, such as creating services, extending interfaces, and maintaining codep.18.
  • OpenAI Harness Engineering: extends this pattern to end-to-end software product development. Engineers define product intent, system architecture, and the rules for continuous integration, code merging, and rollback;Codex Within these constraints, it handles implementation and verification, continuously producing code artifacts that feed into the downstream development process.

10. 3.2.7 How to Evaluate L1 Execution Autonomyp.18

The authors conclude that L1 systems at every layer have The same capability frontier:The AI can carry out increasingly long and consequential improvement processes, and its outputs keep feeding into later stages, but "the process governing improvement is still externally specified".

So evaluating L1 means looking at two Coupling properties:

  • Execution reliability: Whether the AI can correctly carry out a prescribed procedure when conditions change. L1 systems can complete very long action sequences with little human intervention, but they are always bound by pre-written procedures. When task conditions fall outside what a procedure covers, or the procedure's implicit assumptions no longer hold, the system cannot revise the procedure on its own and is prone to failure.
  • Persistent safety:whether errors introduced during execution can be caught before their artifacts propagate to subsequent improvement rounds Before be discovered. Persistence will Amplify the consequences of failure, because artifacts are retained and reused, so errors spill beyond the task that introduced them and affect later iterations. Evaluation therefore needs to check not only whether the process ran correctly, but also whether artifacts are still valid before they move downstream.

Section conclusion (Finding):L1 means executing human-defined improvement processes at scale. Humans first encode mature engineering experience into explicit improvement steps and validation rules, and AI executes them on concrete tasks; the outputs are retained and incorporated into later development, so the same improvement process can be applied repeatedly across unrelated tasks and different development stages.

Put simply: an L1 AI is a highly capable, tireless "executor that follows the SOP." Its contributions accumulate, but it can't decide how the SOP is written, nor can it autonomously choose which improvement to try next. The latter is exactly what the next level, L2 (autonomy over improvement strategy), addresses.

§3.3 L2: Autonomy in Improving Strategy

Key points of this part

  • The core change at L2 is that AI no longer just executes changes prescribed by humans, but instead Decide for itself which improvement to try next based on evaluation feedback. Goals, task boundaries, and acceptance criteria are still set by humans. The authors see this as a partial handover of the research responsibility of "turning observed evidence into the next experiment."
  • The authors do not organize L2 by individual system. Instead, they group it into three categories by "the object the improvement strategy acts on": Prompt Search, Agent and Harness Search, and Model and Training Search. The larger the object, the broader the scope for intervention.
  • High strategy autonomy does not mean strong recursive self-improvement (RSI). Search can be inefficient, can exploit loopholes in the evaluator, and gains may vanish after a few rounds. The authors flag three risks in particular: benchmark overfitting, mistaking compute investment for algorithmic progress, and LLM evaluators sharing blind spots with the proposer.

Note: the L1 wrap-up at the start of p.18 (LinkedIn CAPT, OpenAI Harness Engineering, §3.2.7 L1 evaluation) and the L3 content from p.22 onward (§3.4) are not part of this section and are not covered here.

1. Definition of L2: autonomously deciding "how to change"p.18

The authors define L2 as an AI system that Uses evaluation feedback to choose which improvement intervention to try next, rather than executing a pre-specified update. It proposes changes, tests them, and keeps accepted revisions. Humans remain responsible for three things: the objective, the task boundary, and the acceptance criteria. The outer search framework itself can stay fixed; L2 autonomy shows up only in "how the target system should be improved".

The authors explain why this step matters. In many AI development workflows, executing a change that has already been worked out is relatively mechanical; the hard part is Decide which change is worth trying. Researchers have to make a series of judgments:

  • interpret the causes of failure;
  • Infer which component is limiting progress;
  • Pick an intervention whose effect is uncertain;
  • Decide the next step after seeing the results.

The more interventions there are to choose from, the more this experimental decision-making becomes a bottleneck. Exhaustive search is infeasible, and manual trials are limited by researchers' time and their preconceived intuitions. This is exactly the bottleneck L2 systems aim to automate: the improver decides the next candidate intervention on its own, based on evaluation results and experimental evidence.

Put simply: L1 is like a chef who cooks by following a recipe; L2 is like a chef who tastes the dish and decides for themselves "whether the next batch needs more salt or less oil." The recipe's goal (making a tasty dish) and the judging criteria (who tastes it and how it's scored) are still set by someone else.

The authors argue that this shift increasingly resembles Partial automation of the empirical researcher role. He cites OpenAI's view that current frontier agents, when handling well-defined research tasks under human supervision, have reached the "automated research intern" stage, while priorities, and which results merit further investment or deployment, are still decided by researchersp.18. The authors therefore stress that L2's autonomy Not unrestricted control over the research, only handing off one specific responsibility: turning observed evidence into the next experiment.

The authors summarize the typical L2 loop in six steps:

Observe performance → diagnose → choose how to modify → instantiate and test → keep or roll back → repeat

L2 is therefore organized by "what the policy search acts on," rather than by individual systemsp.19.

3. §3.3.1 Prompt Searchp.19

This is The narrowest L2 scenario. Based on execution traces and evaluation results, the improver decides how to change the instructions (prompts or other textual control variables) that steer the model or agent, rather than just applying a prescribed edit.

  • Industry examples:Dropbox used GEPA to rewrite a prompt. The prompt tells the LLM how to judge whether retrieved files or messages are relevant to a user's search.
  • Method evolution: Early evolutionary methods searched across different mutations; newer reflective optimizers use execution traces to identify failure modes and then generate targeted modifications.
  • GEPA: attributes failures to specific modules. It keeps multiple complementary prompt variants rather than rushing to lock in a single greedy lineage.
  • MPO: extends the editable objects to multimodal prompts, using semantic feedback from evaluation to guide subsequent text and non-text prompt candidates.
  • C-Evolve: embeds candidate generation in a fixed evolutionary protocol.

The takeaway of this subsection: the search mechanism itself can be specified externally, but the specific prompt edits "grow out of" evaluation feedback. The authors stress that increased autonomy The point is not generating the prompts themselves, but rather in "control over which revision strategy is attempted next".

4. §3.3.2 Agent and Harness Searchp.19–20

This category of improvement involves redesigning how reasoning components and tool-calling components work together. When the editable object expands from prompts to Executable agent code, policy search starts to look like "automated system design".

  • Microsoft Foundry's Agent Optimizer: Rewrites the hosted agent's system instructions, skills, and local function tool descriptions based on evaluation failures.
  • ADAS(Automated Design of Agentic Systems): formalizes an agent as a Python forward function, with a meta-agent continually adding new designs to an ever-growing archive of candidate agents. In each iteration, the meta-agent evaluates candidates on validation data, then stores the code and metrics together in the archive.

The authors point out the key difference: these design decisions are written in executable harness code, so A single modification can change the agent's behavior across the entire task, rather than changing just a single instruction the way a prompt does.

The authors then introduce two workflow search systems, which represent the design space in different ways:

  • AFlow: encodes workflows as executable graphs and uses Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to explore code-level modifications step by step, with execution results serving as feedback for subsequent branches.
  • AgentSquare: breaks agent design into reusable modules and searches over them through evolution and recombination. Successful structures found in one configuration can become building blocks for another.

Boundaries: Autonomy at this level goes far beyond local tuning, but in an important sense it is still bounded. The improver chooses among agent designs, yet a candidate is still promoted because of how it performs on Externally maintained evaluation pipeline perform better. The authors stress that L2 autonomy means searching for better system configurations and “does not imply authority to redefine what constitutes successful performance”, i.e., it has no authority to redefine what counts as successp.20.

5. §3.3.3 Model and Training Searchp.20–21

Here the improver has to Empirical evidence obtained during training, into decisions about how the learner itself is configured, constructed, and optimized. The background: there are far too many possible experiments, and human researchers can only pick a tiny fraction to run based on accumulated intuition. The new role of the L2 improver is compress this experimental cycle: Reviews what happened in past runs, infers where improvements might come from, and plans the next experiment accordingly. The authors divide this category into four types.

(1) Configuration Search

  • Automatically allocates expensive training trials.
  • New agentic methods add semantic reasoning over past experiments on top of traditional search. Instead of treating each trial as an isolated black-box query, the system interprets training results and uses them to focus subsequent exploration.
  • Example: NVIDIA's 2026 TAO workflow for post-training Cosmos 3 lets a coding agent invoke LLM-guided AutoML against a fixed validation target. Humans supply the model, task, data and target metrics, and the system handles most of the recurring decision of "which configuration to evaluate next".

(2) Architecture Search

  • Hands over model architecture design, reducing reliance on manually designed search spaces. LLM-based improvers can interpret experimental results and propose not enumerated in advance structural changes.
  • Example: AgentNAS starts from the observation that modern neural architecture search (NAS) still relies on hand-designed, task-specific search spaces. It first uses an LLM to generate a task-specific seed architecture, then derives a structured search space from that design for subsequent combinatorial search.

(3) Training-Rule Search

  • The improver modifies the executable process of "how model parameters are learned". Frontier agents are starting to automate the step "from hypothesis to experiment".
  • Example 1: OpenAI's NanoGPT evaluation for GPT-6 Astra. The agent is given a fixed validation target, One GPU and a constrained training setup, and asks it to diagnose training bottlenecks on its own, modify the training code, adjust configurations, and make useful changes to the training loop.
  • Example 2: The open research environment autoresearch makes this division of labor even more explicit. The data pipeline, evaluation function, metrics and per-experiment compute budget are all fixed, and the agent repeatedly edits the training program,Keep a change only if the protected validation metric improves.

(4) Systems and Implementation Search

  • The improver looks for more efficient implementations of model computation, while external tests guard correctness and system-level performance. The process is: profile bottlenecks → propose implementation changes → verify numerical correctness → measure performance on real hardware → decide what to change next based on the results.
  • Example: Rather than starting from arbitrary code generation, AutoKernel first runs profiling to find the operators with the greatest end-to-end impact, steering the agent's experiments toward these bottlenecks. Candidate Triton or CUDA implementations Must pass correctness checks and show a measured GPU speedup before being acceptedp.20–21.

7. §3.3.4 How to evaluate L2 policy autonomyp.21

1. Autonomy ≠ improvement quality. The authors first stress that greater autonomy over improvement strategies does not by itself mean stronger recursive self-improvement. An L2 system may have a very rich intervention space yet still run into three problems: inefficient search, exploitation of evaluator weaknesses, and gains that vanish after a few iterations. The authors therefore evaluate two things separately:Degree of delegated autonomy, and the quality of the resulting improvement process.

2. The three must work together. These two dimensions are complementary. A wider search space helps only when two conditions both hold: feedback can distinguish good candidates from bad ones, and the search process can explore the space efficiently. The authors use this to explain why current industrial systems are concentrated in Coding, model training, kernel optimization These domains: candidate interventions in these domains can be executed directly, and feedback arrives quickly.

3. Evaluation risks introduced by adaptive search loops. The same loop also creates typical evaluation pitfalls:

  • Benchmark overfitting: Repeatedly accessing the development set can easily cause the system to overfit the benchmark.
  • Treating compute as progress: extra search compute can be mistaken for algorithmic improvement.
  • Shared blind spots:An LLM evaluator may share the same blind spots as the proposer.

Public automated research systems have already made these risks concrete. Reported behaviors include cherry-picking random seeds, finding shortcuts, and repeatedly querying the evaluator to try to extract test labels 〔20〕.

A key judgment by the authors: once a benchmark is adaptively queried over and over, it effectively becomes Part of the optimization target, rather than a passive measurement tool. Put simply: once test questions are practiced on repeatedly, they no longer truly measure ability.

4. Attitude toward enterprise reports. Results reported by the companies themselves can serve as evidence of "feasibility and scale," but their sources should be stated, and they should not be treated as equivalent to independently reproduced experiments.

8. Section findingsp.21

The authors summarize:L2 shifts human work from "proposing each specific improvement" to "constraining the search for improvements". Humans still set the goals and acceptance criteria, but no longer need to pick each candidate intervention themselves. The bottleneck thus shifts from "executing a known improvement" to "searching among possible improvements."

This sets up L3 in the next section: L2 decides "how to change," while L3 further decides on its own "what experience to learn from next"p.22.

§3.4 L3: Autonomy over Experience for Future Learning

Key points of this part

  • On top of L2's "deciding for itself how to improve", L3 adds another layer of autonomy: based on its current capabilities, failures, and learning history, the system decides What experience to learn from next. What is learned in turn changes the next round's choices, closing the loop.
  • Industry data pipelines are highly automated, but "operational automation" is not the same as "experiential autonomy". Whether a system reaches L3 depends on whether there is a closed dependency chain: learner state → experience acquisition → persistent learning → subsequent acquisition decisions.
  • Representative approaches fall into two types: ① adaptive task generation and self-play; ② autonomous practice in the environment. Evaluation should separate "whether it is autonomous" from "how well it learns," and watch for "experience corruption" propagating across rounds.

1. Definition of L3: control over the "learning agenda"

p.22 What L2 cares about is How improvement; the new autonomy added at L3 targets Learning agenda(learning agenda): the system uses evidence about its own capabilities, failures, and learning history to decide what experience to learn from next. After learning, the system's state changes, and what experience to acquire in the next round depends on that state. As a result, "selecting experience" and "durable improvement" are linked across rounds.

The authors summarize the typical loop at this layer as: observe learner state → select learning experiences → acquire experiences → update persistent state → reshape future experiences → repeat.

Defining characteristics is "learner-conditioned future experience acquisition". It has two specific requirements:

  • Evidence about the learner's current state must influence the decision of "which experiences to select, generate, or seek out next";
  • The resulting persistent updates must in turn shape subsequent acquisition decisions.

This decision can be handed to a separate curriculum component or built into the agent's own policy. The authors highlight a counterexample: after a policy update, the agent happens to visit different states, which does not by itself count Own the learning agenda. In plain terms: it must be "deliberately choosing what to practice next based on where you're weak," not "drifting somewhere else as a side effect of changing."

p.22 A few clarifications:

  • Experience does not have to be generated from scratch.Repeatedly selecting from an externally given data pool qualifies as L3, as long as the selection adapts as the learner changes and is part of an ongoing loop. One-off filtering or retention does not count.
  • Human-designed parts can be retained.The goal, evaluator, update process, and even the experience-selection rules can all be designed by humans. The key question is whether the learner's feedback can autonomously change the next round's learning agenda, without humans redesigning it after every round.
  • Substrate of persistent learning Can be model parameters or reusable external state, such as memories and skills.
  • L3 evidence is only one dimension.When assigning an overall level to a system, the requirements of the other layers in the hierarchy must also be taken into account.

2. From industrial automation to experiential autonomy (§3.4.1)

  1. Data source preparation
  2. Data annotation
  3. Data selection
  4. Data construction

p.22 The main argument proceeds in three steps:

  • which are already automated.Operations such as crawling, filtering, and sample generation can be automated at scale once inputs and acceptance criteria are defined [22, 137, 139].
  • which are still decided by researchers.These reports also describe choices made by researchers: data policy, generation and mixing strategies, training stages, and experimental evaluation [22, 138, 141]. p.22–23 The case studies that follow fall into two patterns: adaptive task generation and self-play, and autonomous practice through environment interaction. The authors note that these are two complementary organizing perspectives, not an exhaustive or mutually exclusive taxonomy: interactive agents can also generate their own tasks, and learner-conditioned selection can happen in either pattern or be applied directly to an existing data pool.

4. Adaptive task generation and self-play (§3.4.2)

p.23 Why is adaptation needed?The value of a training task changes as the learner improves. A fixed task distribution does not explicitly track this moving "competence frontier": problems the learner has mastered no longer provide information, and problems far beyond its ability yield no usable learning signal. Put simply, problems should always sit "just within reach."

The authors identify three major challenges here:

  1. Obtain an informative signal;
  2. Turn signals into a useful task distribution;
  3. ensure the resulting experience is valid and correct.

Representative systems are as follows:

  • SSP(Search Self-Play)[98]p.23: Makes the proposer's reward depend on the solver's current performance. When the solver changes, the signal driving the search for new problems changes too, so the curriculum evolves on its own without manual redesign of each batch. This connects Challenge 1 and Challenge 2.
  • AZR(Absolute Zero Reasoner)[99]: On The same model both poses and solves executable reasoning tasks. Task generation is guided by a "learnability reward" that depends on the solver's performance; a code executor checks both whether the task itself is valid and whether the solution is correct. This separates two requirements that unconstrained synthesis tends to conflate: experience must be Evaluable, while its difficulty must also be Useful to the current learner.
  • R-Zero[103]p.23–24: Uses a Challenger–Solver architecture that requires no external answer labels. The Challenger's "uncertainty reward" depends on how consistent the current Solver's multiple answers are, so whenever the Solver updates, the signal driving later question generation changes too. The authors caution that consistency is only a proxy for "model-perceived difficulty" and directly proves neither that answers are correct nor that there will be future learning gains. Its value is that it keeps experience acquisition responsive to the learner even when no stronger correctness signal is available.
  • STP[100]p.24:formal domains offer another path to reliable feedback. To address sparse proof rewards, STP trains a conjecturer and a prover together. Conjectures that the current prover can "barely prove" are used to train the conjecturer, while checked proofs are used to improve the prover; as the prover's capability frontier shifts, it in turn reshapes subsequent conjectures. There is a division of labor here: the proof assistant handles correctness checking within the formal domain, while learner-dependent selection criteria handle something else, namely finding the right training difficulty.
  • PSV(Propose, Solve, Verify)[101]: applies a similar mechanism to verifiable code generation. Difficulty labels derived from the solver are attached to examples and used to prompt the generation of new specifications; candidate specifications must first pass a compilability check, and generated solutions must be formally verified against the specification before being used for training. The authors note that these checks can only establish properties "relative to the specification"; they cannot guarantee that the specification itself is a worthwhile task, nor that its solutions will improve the learner. PSV's own report shows that problems labeled easy, medium, and hard,Actual difficulty overlaps heavily, showing that generating problems conditioned on difficulty can only achieve Partial rather than exact curriculum control.
  • VisPlay[142]: extends this idea to vision-language reasoning with an image-grounded Questioner and Reasoner. The Questioner's uncertainty reward favors questions for which the confidence of the Reasoner's majority answer is close to a preset midpoint, while a diversity penalty prevents repeated questions. As the Reasoner changes, its answer consistency changes, which in turn changes the question-generation reward. As with R-Zero, this signal scales, but it may reflect question ambiguity or answer confusion rather than necessarily "productive difficulty."

p.24–25 Cross-comparison: Feedback in these systems varies along two interrelated dimensions.

  • Reliability of feedback.Executable results and formal proof checking can establish correctness within a bounded task setting; answer consistency can only estimate difficulty and cannot establish correctness on its own.
  • Where adaptation happens.Can reside in a trained question-generation model, or in generation prompts and selection pipelines that adapt to current measurements of the learner.

These choices trade off verification coverage, feedback cost, and sensitivity to imperfect proxy metrics. The shared advance is:The estimated learning value shapes future experience acquisition, and persistent learning in turn changes this estimate in later rounds.

6. Autonomous practice through environment interaction (§3.4.3)

p.25 Interactive agents must decide where to spend their next unit of learning effort. Simply collecting more trajectories doesn't solve this: unguided exploration keeps practicing behaviors the agent has already mastered, while fixed practice schedules can't explicitly respond to newly learned skills or persistent failures. The three systems below connect interaction feedback to future practice targets.

  • VOYAGER[84]: Combines an automatic curriculum with a persistent library of executable skills in Minecraft. The current state and exploration history determine new goals, and successful behaviors become reusable skills that later tasks can call. Learning changes what the agent "can try," and new exploration provides experience for acquiring new skills. The authors note in particular that the persistence here lies in External skills, without requiring updates to the underlying language model's parameters.
  • SIMA 2[24]: Shows that the experimental setup is critical to identifying this mechanism. Its fixed-task experiments show that self-generated trajectories lead to improvement, but that alone does not show it controls task selection. In the full ASKA self-improvement setup, a Gemini-based task setter uses evaluations from a downstream reward model to focus practice on weaker skills, and the resulting experience is used to train the agent, linking capability assessment to the allocation of later practice. The authors argue that this autonomy belongs, task setter included, to the The entire coupled learning system, without requiring the model that executes actions to make every curriculum decision itself.
  • SEAgent[61]p.25–26: Targets practice in unfamiliar software by explicitly linking "trajectory evaluation" and "curriculum memory." A World State Model provides trajectory judgments and descriptions of GUI state changes to a Curriculum Generator, which updates a persistent software "guidebook" and generates subsequent tasks from it. The Actor learns from the collected experience, and later interactions further revise the guidebook and task set. The guidebook carries information from early exploration into later practice decisions, letting the curriculum extend to previously unexplored operations.

p.26 Comparison of the three:they share the same feedback structure but rely on different persistent resources and sources of judgment.

  • VOYAGER relies on exploration history and a skill library;
  • SIMA 2 relies on reward-model evaluation;
  • SEAgent relies on continuously evolving descriptions of software behavior.

Accordingly, unreliable skill construction, inaccurate reward judgments, and faulty guideline entries can all steer later learning effort in the wrong direction. Autonomous interaction reduces the need for human-designed curricula, but the quality of feedback and accumulated state remains central to whether this loop is reliable.

7. How to evaluate L3 experiential autonomy (§3.4.4)

p.26 First, autonomy does not mean learning well.A system can control its own learning agenda and still acquire experience that is uninformative, too narrow, or wrongly evaluated. Evaluation must therefore establish two things: that the feedback mechanism actually exists, and that using it actually brings benefits. Specifically, it must answer three questions: Which learner signal influenced acquisition? What persistent state did learning change? How did that change affect later acquisition decisions?

Second, use controlled experiments to isolate the "learner-conditioned" contribution.

  • Two controls are available: feeding in the learner state from the acquisition mechanism Frozen at an early checkpoint; or replace adaptive decisions with A fixed plan unrelated to the learner.
  • Comparisons should match access to data sources and environments and keep compute and interaction budgets comparable, including the cost of generating and evaluating experience.
  • The authors specifically caution:The two sides should not get exactly the same samples, otherwise it would eliminate the very "distribution adaptation" being studied.
  • The purpose of these controls is to separate "the value of adaptively acquiring experience" from "gains that come simply from more training resources."

Third, distinguish correctness, difficulty, and learning gain.Formal or executable checks can establish specified properties within the settings they support; consistency measures and model-based judgments are only fallible estimates. Neither "verified correct" nor "estimated to be of appropriate difficulty" alone proves marginal learning value. Where possible, compare acquisition signals against independent validity checks, actual difficulty, and downstream learning gains, and test whether these relationships hold as the learner changes.

Fourth, experience corruption.The authors use this term for the risk that "flawed experience or feedback distorts subsequent learning and acquisition decisions." Examples fall into three categories:

  • Misleading difficulty proxies bias selection toward unsuitable tasks;
  • Inaccurate reviews reward the wrong behavior;
  • Persistent memory carries wrong assumptions into future practice.

Because these effects can enter parameters, skills, memory, or curriculum state, they can Cross-round propagation. The authors note that this is a structural risk of feedback loops, not a claim that every system will show such amplification. Put simply: a single mistake isn't the real danger. The danger is when the mistake gets stored, and the next round generates tasks and practices based on it, drifting further and further off course.

Fifth, trustworthy evaluation requires the following:

  • Track performance across multiple rounds;
  • Check the validity and diversity of the acquired experience;
  • Tests transfer to new tasks and environments;
  • Strictly separate "feedback used to guide the curriculum" from "protected tests used to evaluate generalization". If test instances are repeatedly exposed through task selection, reward construction, or persistent memory, they become part of the learning process;
  • For task-generation systems, check whether the curriculum keeps tracking the learner rather than collapsing onto narrow or easy-to-score examples;
  • For interactive systems, check whether practice keeps producing new skills rather than staying with familiar behaviors.

Finding:L3 adds autonomy over the future learning agenda. The system uses its evolving capabilities, failures, or learning history to guide what experience to select, generate, or seek next, and persistent learning feeds back into subsequent acquisition decisions. Human-designed objectives, evaluators, and learning rules can remain in place; experiential autonomy refers to decisions made within this framework, and it does not by itself guarantee improvement is useful or reliable.

§3.5 L4: Autonomy in Deployment and Environment Adaptation

Key points of this part

  • L4 concerns what happens after an agent goes live. Based on interaction feedback from real deployments, the system decides on its own which experience to consolidate and use to persistently change itself, including memory, skills, code, the harness, or even weights. L3 asks "what experience to learn next"; L4 asks "which consequences of experience should remain."
  • The authors group L4 work into three types of mechanisms: trajectory distillation, iterative revision of agent systems, and selective retention and deployment of updates. Table 6 summarizes 24 representative systems, benchmarks, or analyses across these three types.
  • The typical risk at this layer is "persistent update failure": a bad change that has been accepted can affect many later decisions before it is exposed. Goals, access boundaries, protected evaluations, and release authority remain in human hands.

1. Definition and core loop of L4

Definitionp.26. At L4, the AI uses interaction feedback from continuous deployment to decide how a running agent should adapt persistently. The authors deliberately distinguish this from L3: L3 focuses on "what experience to acquire for learning," while L4 focuses on "how experience during operation changes the memory, skills, or execution components used by later tasks."

Boundariesp.27. Retained changes shape later behavior and also change the feedback available later. Four things remain externally governed: the objective, access boundaries, protected evaluation, and the authority to decide on significant releases.

Core loopp.27. The authors summarize the loop at this layer as:

Observe deployment interactions → propose persistent adaptations → revise Agent components → validate and retain → reuse in subsequent tasks → collect new feedback, repeat

The three types of mechanisms differ in three ways: what gets changed, when it gets reused, and how its continued usefulness is confirmed. Table 6 is organized along these three dimensions.

2. Figure 7: Linking Three Types of Mechanisms Through a "Pagination Bug"

scenario. A deployed Agent performs real tasks in web or API environments. The state it can persistently modify includes memory, skills/tools, and code/harness, with weights marked as "optional". User needs change. One task required "collecting all issues across all pages", but the Agent requested only page 1 and stopped, producing incomplete results. It was later corrected to: following next_page keep requesting until it is empty to collect all pages. The same correction recurred in later tasks.

Step 1: Trajectory distillation (Metis-style). Tasks A, B, and C all use "follow next_page". The system merges these experiences into a shared procedure, then compiles it into a reusable tool fetch_all_pages(api). The tool must first be compiled and run in a sandbox, and is stored only after passing checks; later, task D calls it directly. The figure summarizes this as "multiple experiences → one reusable artifact." A skill is also distilled: "check pagination before finishing."

Step 2: Agent system revision (HarnessDev-style). The current harness v1 fetches only one page, so a candidate v2 is proposed that loops in the harness to update the page pointer. Development-stage run results are fed back for further revisions. A separate channel replays frozen hidden tasks on a fixed executor, measuring success rate and executor token cost. This channel only measures; its results do not flow back to the reviser.

Step 3: Selective retention and deployment (HDSO-style). First, state a hypothesis: "incomplete results will decrease." Then run a controlled comparison on the same set of evaluation tasks: the control group uses only approved skills, and the treatment group uses "approved skills + candidate." After comparing results, gradually expand the paired tests, and finally confirm on independent tasks. If the conclusion holds, add the candidate to the approved library; if it does not, or is unclear, reject it and keep the status quo.

Put simply, Figure 7 shows three ways the same lesson can be put into practice: turned into a tool, used to improve the framework, or formally adopted only after a controlled experiment.

4. §3.5.1 Trajectory distillation

Definitionp.27. Trajectory distillation compresses an agent's interaction history into compact artifacts that persist across sessions and influence later tasks. Compared with raw trajectories, these artifacts are more concise, cheaper to consult, and structurally easier to reuse. The authors group them into four categories by artifact form.

1. Textual experience memoryp.27–28. The artifacts are natural-language passages that are retrieved into the prompt for later tasks.

  • Dynamic Cheatsheet. lets a frozen model get progressively stronger on a series of related problems without ground-truth labels. It maintains an evolving notebook of strategies, code snippets, and known pitfalls. The same model consults this notebook when answering and is also responsible for curating it, adding newly distilled lessons and deleting outdated entries.
  • Agentic Context Engineering (ACE). Follows the same idea, but focuses on keeping memory detailed as it grows. It stores short entries, each with a counter recording whether that entry helped or hurt in later tasks. Updates apply only small patches instead of rewriting the whole thing, which avoids the information loss caused by compression.
  • ReasoningBank. Applies this idea to multi-step agents, learning from failures as well as successes. Each judged trajectory is distilled into a short, titled strategy, which is retrieved before subsequent tasks begin.

2. Structured memoryp.28–29. Experience is stored as explicit records or graphs, so "when to reuse it" becomes inspectable.

  • APEX. The goal is to keep the Agent exploring new strategies as its memory grows, rather than sticking to familiar routines. It builds a strategy map: nodes are task milestones, and edges are prerequisite dependencies. After each episode, the outcome is propagated back along the milestones that led to it, so the map records which paths work. A separate step adds branches that are "promising but not yet tried." During planning, the Agent consults this map and chooses between known good paths and untried ones.
  • A user-centric structure. PersonaAgent turns a user's episodic and semantic memory into a per-user persona prompt. When PAHF finds no relevant preference and the request is ambiguous, it proactively asks the user to clarify, then merges the feedback into memory and revises outdated entries.
  • MemToolAgent. gives memory a more procedural role: it stores critiques of failed tool calls and adjusts how many entries are retrieved per call.

3. Procedural skill librariesp.29. Rather than just describing experience, it extracts skills or standard operating procedures (SOPs) that can be followed directly.

  • Trace2Skill. First, a frozen agent runs on domain tasks to produce trajectories. Two analyst roles then read the trajectories, one focusing on errors and the other on successes, and each proposes skill edits. These edits are merged in stages into a portable skill catalog. Because the merged document is read directly rather than retrieved on every turn, later agents only need to load it once as instructions.
  • PRACTICE. Applies offline distillation to embodied agents while keeping the skill library consistent as it changes, without unbounded growth. It uses a trainable skill learner that proposes four kinds of edits in batches: add, refine, merge, and delete. Training has three stages: first, it learns to generate skills and maintain the library from oracle trajectories; then, it compares successful and failed trajectories of different executors on the same task to learn failure awareness; finally, it is distilled toward a stronger teacher model on its own edit distribution.
  • Two ways to acquire skills during execution. PANDO improves web agents during deployment by distilling two things from each execution: rules that prevent repeated failures, and parameterized routines that can replace multi-step browser subgoals. PILOT uses a supervisor to distill procedures and failure modes from live long-horizon runs, so later sessions start with accumulated skills.

4. Executable artifactsp.29. Compiles experience into directly callable code, combining the flexibility of textual memory with the efficiency of tools.

  • Metis. Uses dual memory: a text store for plans, environment facts and pitfalls, and a code store for callable tools. Text entries are maintained by a reflector. Frequently reused text plans are rewritten as tools, but are accepted only if they compile and run correctly in a sandbox. Retrieval covers both text entries and tool descriptions.
  • Evo-Harness. Extends the target from a single tool to the entire harness. It compiles lessons from failed or negatively rated tasks into cross-task patterns and task-specific procedures.
  • SHAPER. Designed for embodied agents, it co-evolves two things: textual planning guidance, and a sandboxed Python function that decides which context the planner sees.

Evaluation methodp.29. Evaluation in this category varies in time horizon and in "what counts as a gain":

  • Evo-Memory restructures datasets into sequential task streams to measure memory accumulation and reuse over long time horizons.
  • PANDO audits behavior within a single run, reporting action repetition rate, extra steps, and prompt cache utilization.
  • Metis measures task quality against execution cost and build cost.

5. §3.5.2 Iterative Revision of Agent Systems

This category modifies the agent system itselfp.29, rather than what it has memorized. Modifiable targets include the skills that produce solutions, the rubrics that judge them, the surrounding harness that runs them, and the underlying weights. The authors argue that the key question here is which components may be changed, and which acceptance criteria must stay fixed while the changes are made.

1. Co-evolving componentsp.29. Revising two coupled components at once introduces circular reasoning: if the judge and the solver change together, a higher score may simply mean the judge has become more lenient, not that the solutions have improved.

DecoEvo solution is to evolve both "solving skills" and "rubric-generation skills" in text space while decoupling their objectives, and to provide no ground-truth rubrics during optimization.

  • Solving skills are updated based on per-criterion feedback.
  • The rubric generator must pass two score-independent audits: a structural audit checks whether the rubric covers the task requirements, and a contrastive audit checks whether it can distinguish between responses with close scores. A Pareto check is also applied across the two audits.

Because the generator never sees the solver's total score, it cannot inflate its own performance by "lowering the bar." Put simply, the question setter is never told the test-takers' scores, so it has no incentive to make the questions easier.

2. Benchmarks for autonomous revisionp.29–30. This kind of work doesn't propose new methods; instead, it measures whether existing models can actually revise their own systems.

  • HarnessDev. Tests whether a model can create and improve its own harness. Here, the harness means the scaffolding around the model: prompts, tool loops, and execution logic. A creator agent starts from a minimal seed and a few development examples, builds a complete harness, and keeps revising it based on downstream feedback. Candidate versions are frozen and evaluated on hidden tasks for both success rate and executor token cost. The creator and executor are kept separate, so different harnesses can be compared fairly on the same fixed executor.
  • ASPIRE. Tests whether a model can improve itself given only a vague goal. How to select data, which update method to use, and what validation signal to rely on are all left to the agent, and the scope covers both weights and the harness. A score-gated controller serves as a safeguard: any change that does not improve the validation score is rolled back.
  • S3Gym. Restricts the problem to the experience channel. It provides no verifier results, so the agent must judge on its own whether a trajectory is good or bad. Under this condition, it compares three improvement routes: raw history, compressed summaries, and parameter training.

3. Revision gain analysisp.30. Whether "revised" means "benefited" is itself an open question. One analysis argues the two are often conflated.

"Harness Updating Is Not Harness Benefit" separates two capabilities: producing useful persistent updates, and actually using those updates when solving tasks. The experiments use a fixed "solve–evolve" pipeline with identical prompts and budgets, covering multiple base models and three agent benchmarks. There are three conclusions:

  • Update capability is largely independent of the strength of the base model. Updates produced by small models can deliver gains comparable to those from frontier models.
  • Which capabilities benefit from the updated framework varies non-monotonically across models. Failures fall into two patterns: the relevant artifacts are never activated, or they are activated but not faithfully executed.
  • Practical implications. Capability investment should go into the agent that solves the problems, not the module responsible for evolution.

6. §3.5.3 Selective retention and deployment of updates

This category focuses on what happens after a change is proposedp.30. Trajectory distillation and system revision both ultimately produce only a "candidate change." This section asks which candidates can become persistent state and which remain available to later tasks. The authors discuss this control at three points: before admission, during ongoing use, and at release to production systems.

1. Candidate validationp.30. An update must provide evidence before it can enter persistent state.

  • HDSO. The goal is to guard against two kinds of bad skills: spurious shortcuts distilled from noisy trajectories, and rules the executor simply cannot follow. The approach: a curator model observes condensed executor trajectories, proposes a hypothesis, and attaches an explicit verification plan. Each candidate runs the same batch of tasks twice: once with only the currently approved skill library, as the control group, and once with the candidate skill added, as the treatment group. A candidate enters the approved library only if the difference between the two results supports the hypothesis. Comparisons proceed in stages at increasing scale, with final confirmation on independent tasks.
  • A similar gate in Metis. Its threshold applies after a text plan has already entered memory: a text plan must recur across multiple tasks and pass dependency and compilation checks before it is promoted to executable code.

2. Library maintenancep.30. After admission, attention shifts to the health of the skill library itself, because skills that were once useful can degrade as tasks and models change.

Library Drift The study finds that unchecked accumulation gradually degrades retrieval quality and stalls progress, and that this degradation often occurs before it shows up in task scores. It proposes three-part lifecycle management:

  • Append-only evidence log. tracks each skill's contribution to task outcomes and retires a skill once its contribution drops below a certain level.
  • The number of active skills is capped. New skills must compete with existing entries for slots.
  • a meta-skill. New skills are written according to a stored guide that specifies how future skills should be written.

The study also shows that the choice of governance matters: retiring skills too aggressively performs even worse than no management at all. Another analysis (Rethinking Skill Evolution) found that carefully filtering skill modifications improves task performance more than repeatedly rewriting skills based on task outcomes.

3. Governed deploymentp.30–31. In production, a bad update affects real users, so strict gates are needed.

Tax AI is a production tax-filing system whose improvements are driven by corrections from tax practitioners:

  • Corrections are recorded as structured field-level evidence, and recurring failures are consolidated into evaluation targets.
  • The coding agent investigates execution traces, evals, and the code repository, implements fixes, and verifies them with targeted and regression evals.
  • The loop can only modify bounded parts of the product: the extraction schema, data source selection, tax engine mappers, and scorers. Decisions on architecture and product design remain with engineers.
  • Each fix is submitted as a pull request and released only after engineering review. Cases the agent cannot solve are handed back to practitioners.

The final acceptance decision is therefore made by humans, and the authors count the delay introduced by review as part of the loop's operating cost.

7. §3.5.4 How to evaluate L4 deployment autonomy

Degree of autonomy is not the same as quality of improvementp.31. A higher degree of autonomous adaptation after deployment does not, on its own, prove that improvement is reliable. An L4 system may retain many updates without showing that they remain useful when tasks, environments or executors change. Evaluations should treat "degree of deployment autonomy" and "quality of the resulting adaptation process" separately.

Typical risk: persistent update failurep.31. An accepted change may already have influenced many later decisions before its weaknesses come to light. Across the various forms of retained state, failures can have four causes:

  • Drew the wrong lessons from noisy evidence;
  • The lesson itself is correct, but it is applied outside its valid scope;
  • a relevant update exists but is not invoked;
  • It gains new behaviors but sacrifices existing capabilities.

Final task scores alone cannot distinguish these four mechanisms. The authors argue that credible evaluation must link each retained change to three things: the evidence that produced it, how it was later used, and its impact on new tasks and on previously solved tasks.

Uncertainty in existing evidencep.31. Whether deployment-time adaptation stays effective over the long term is still unclear:

  • Most evaluations cover only task streams of limited length and limited combinations of models, tasks and environments; repeated evaluation also makes longer-term research expensive.
  • Feedback may be incomplete or generated inside the loop: outcome labels may be noisy, the evaluator may change along with the system, and useful retained artifacts may fail to influence execution.

So L4 evaluation should examine adaptation across time and distribution shifts, including "from which point a retained change starts altering subsequent behavior".

Boundaries of L4p.31. L4 autonomy is still constrained by the surrounding deployment process. The agent can decide how interaction evidence changes persistent state, while goals, access boundaries, protected evaluations, and release authority are still set by humans. In the original paper's words, L4 is about "autonomy over operational adaptation within a deployed system".

Section conclusion (Finding)p.31. L4 moves autonomy from "choosing experience" to "deciding which consequences of experience persist in deployment." The agent turns interaction history into retained changes to memory, skills, harness, code, or model parameters, and these changes affect later tasks and later feedback. Humans retain the goals, protected acceptance criteria, access boundaries, and final say over significant releases.

§3.6 L5: Meta-Improvement

Key points of this part

  • Definition of L5:The threshold for L5 (meta-improvement) is that the AI does not just improve "the task itself" but persistently modifies "the mechanism responsible for producing future improvements", and actually uses the modified mechanism in subsequent rounds. This mechanism can be an improver, evaluator, search policy, or research strategy. The authors divide L5 into two tiers:Structural L5 Only shows that the change was inherited and controlled the next round;Effective L5 It must also show that the change actually produces better successors under the same budget.
  • Three classes of representative systems: these respectively improve the search process (STOP, Gödel Agent, DGM, HyperAgents), the successor evaluator (RQGM), and research goals and strategies (A-Evolve-Training). Industry examples include AIRA2, AAR, and AIDE2. Current evidence supports only "bounded meta-improvement". Cumulative, statistically reliable cross-generation speedup under comparable resources remains unproven.
  • Evaluation and limitations: Evaluations must distinguish between "a currently stronger agent" and "a process that reliably produces stronger successors." The authors call for mechanism audits, matched-budget controls, and transfer tests with the mechanism held fixed. Humans always retain control of the overall goals, protected acceptance criteria, and resource permissions.

1. Why L5 is needed: the improvement process itself becomes the bottleneck p.31

The authors first use two examples to show that the improvement process itself may be the bottleneck:

  • A coding agent repeatedly generates similar failing patches because its search process discards useful candidates.
  • A research agent keeps optimizing its dev-set score even after that score no longer predicts external performance.

Fixing such problems requires modifying "the program that directs experiments and judges results." But designing such programs is costly: whether a modification is good can only be judged by the improvements it later produces, and this often only becomes clear across multiple tasks and repeated runsp.31.

2. The boundary of L5 and closing the loop p.31–32

When L5 begins:The AI persistently modifies a mechanism "responsible for future improvement" and uses the modified version in subsequent rounds. Editable mechanisms include the improver, the successor evaluator, the search policy, and programs that guide research.

The authors compare it side by side with the previous levelsp.31:

  • L2 searches candidate interventions;
  • L3 decides what experience to acquire;
  • L4 brings deployment feedback into persistent adaptation;
  • L5 goes a step further, making "the procedure that governs subsequent improvement" itself the object of improvement.

The paper's distinguishing example: a deployed agent uses Constant the learning program retains new debugging skills, which is L4; if what it modifies and reuses is "the program that diagnoses failures and builds these skills", it may cross into L5.

Put simply: L4 is "learning a new skill"; L5 is "improving its own method for learning skills, and actually using it the next time."

When the loop is closed: the modified mechanism in turn governs how later successors are generated, evaluated, or selected. Inherited state includes accepted code, prompts, evaluators, or research strategies, plus the evidence needed for later revisions.

Human designers remain responsible for setting the overall mission, protected evaluations, editable components, and resource permissions, and may also retain veto and deployment rights. Some of these decisions are enforced by fixed infrastructure and don't require human approval each roundp.31–32.

Structural L5 and effective L5p.32:

  • Structural L5(structural L5): proving that an AI-led change was persistently retained and controlled a later round of improvement.
  • Effective L5(effective L5): demonstrates, under comparable budgets and independent evaluation, that the modified mechanism actually produces or selects better successors.

The authors also give two specific rules for judgment:

  • "Self-modifying task code" alone is not enough. If the process responsible for later revisions does not itself change, it does not count as L5.
  • Conversely, an evolved successor evaluator can constitute structural L5 even if the task agent's code is completely unchanged.

So whether something "is L5" has to be judged with respect to The specific mechanism under examination to judge, rather than labeling the whole system.

6. §3.6.2 Improving successor evaluation p.33

This category lets AI improve the evaluators that "decide which successors are kept." There is a tension here:

  • Fixed evaluator: repeated optimization concentrates pressure on the judge's blind spots, favoring proposals that exploit its errors.
  • Freely modified evaluator: before-and-after scores may reflect shifting standards and lose comparability.

Red Queen Gödel Machine (RQGM)This tension is handled with "evaluator co-evolution":

  • The learned evaluator is frozen within an epoch.
  • At a predetermined boundary, an independent ground-truth anchor is used to compare the challenger evaluators, and the selected one governs the next cycle.
  • All scores that depend on the replaced evaluator are invalidated, and affected agents are re-evaluated the next time they are visited.

In plain terms: you can change referees mid-game, but the new referee must first be tested against an agreed-upon answer key; once the referee is changed, all scores given by the old referee are voided and re-scored.

Results: on held-out Polyglot coding tasks, the preprint reports a pass rate 71.7%, compared with HGM-H's 69.9%, and the search consumes fewer tokens.

Limitations: the anchors, replacement schedule, and orchestration logic are all fixed externally, and they provide the reference for judging changes to the evaluator. The paper's formal stability argument holds only within each frozen period Internal holds, but does not apply when the goal can be changed arbitrarily.

1. §3.7 Cross-level synthesis: autonomy levels as the "gradual handover of improvement responsibility"p.35

The authors first offer a unified view: B0 through L5 can be seen as Responsibility in the improvement loop is handed over from humans to AI step by step. The levels differ mainly not in "which algorithm is used" or "which artifact is optimized," but in three questions:

  1. which improvement decisions the AI makes itself;
  2. which state is carried over to later rounds;
  3. which acceptance conditions remain externally protected and cannot be changed by the AI.

In plain terms: to tell which level a system belongs to, ask "who signs off on this round's improvement, whether the result can persist, and who decides what counts as passing", not what technology it uses.

Boundary between adjacent levelsp.35, each of which the authors summarize with one keyword:

  • B0 → L1: persistence. Adopted changes must persist after the current task ends rather than being discarded once it is done.
  • L1 → L2: handing over the choice of strategy. AI no longer just executes improvement methods specified by humans; it decides for itself which intervention to try.
  • L2 → L3: taking control of the future learning agenda. The evolving learner begins to influence "what experience to acquire next," i.e., it decides for itself what to learn.
  • L3 → L4: moving into continuous deployment and interaction with the environment. The feedback process extends into the real operating environment, so the system's changes must remain useful under changing operating conditions.
  • L4 → L5: recursive inheritance. The mechanism responsible for subsequent improvements itself also becomes something that is improved and inherited.

Level does not equal qualityp.35. The authors explicitly caution that a higher autonomy level does not by itself mean a better improvement process. Granting AI more permissions can easily come with the following problems:

  • Low search efficiency;
  • Feedback is unreliable;
  • capability regression;
  • Evaluator exploitation;
  • Poor transferability.

The authors therefore separate two dimensions: how much improvement responsibility the AI has internalized, and the quality and efficiency of the resulting improvements. Put simply, "having more authority" and "doing a good job" are different things and should be evaluated separately.

Distribution of existing evidencep.35:

  • Low and mid levels: broadly supported by existing systems.
  • L3–L4: capabilities depend more on the specific domain.
  • End-to-end L5: evidence is concentrated in bounded prototype systems and in emerging industrial or research systems.

Because domains differ so much, the authors analyze each application domain separately in the next chapter.

3. §4.1 RSI in scientific domainsp.36

Why it matters: Scientific progress is not just about solving individual problems, but also about improving "how hypotheses are generated, tested, and revised in the future." This makes science an especially important application area for RSI.

Typical loop: Updates the AI4Sci system using experimental evidence or falsification results. The specific steps are:

  1. The research agent proposes hypotheses;
  2. run computational or physical experiments;
  3. Attribute the observed results;
  4. Retains verified changes, which can target the generation policy, tools, skills, memory, or scaffold.

Representative systems:

  • HypoForge: distills review comments and execution results into reusable skills for generating and testing hypotheses.
  • Test-Time Tool Evolution: Synthesizes and verifies executable scientific tools for later reuse.
  • DrugSAGE: transfers verified modeling workflows and error-recovery strategies to other tasks.
  • SIA: extends the editable scope to agent scaffolding, tools, search logic, and even model weights.

The authors' assessment: These systems do demonstrate persistent improvement of scientific agent components, but their evaluators, update selection rules, and promotion criteria are largely fixed. The authors also note that optimizing a single molecule, equation, or hypothesis is only "RSI-adjacent" and does not show that the scientific agent itself has become stronger. Put simply, writing a good paper doesn't mean the researcher has improved; what matters is whether the researcher's own methods have improved and been retained.

4. §4.2 RSI in embodied intelligencep.36–37

Characteristics: Improvement is grounded in physical interaction and causal feedback.

Typical loop:

  1. Select or generate experience;
  2. execution policy;
  3. Trajectories obtained from evaluation;
  4. Commits verified updates to the components used in the next interaction, including the policy, world model, skill library, harness, or curriculum.

Representative systems:

  • POET: Lets the environment and the agent co-evolve. Newly generated challenges both expose gaps in current capabilities and drive those capabilities to expand.
  • Voyager: converts successful behaviors into an executable skill library for later exploration.
  • World-VLA-Loop:Alternates between two things: improving the world model based on the policy's failures, and optimizing the policy within the improved model.

These works go beyond “within-episode error correction” toward persistent co-adaptation between agents and their environments.

Three main difficultiesp.37:

  1. Experience is endogenous: What experience gets collected depends on the current policy itself, so it is easily biased by that policy's limitations.
  2. Failures are hard to attribute:it is hard to tell whether a failure lies in perception, planning, control, or hardware.
  3. Real-world trials are costly: Real-world trials are expensive and cannot be safely "redone."

As a result, most reported loops rely on simulation, or on externally specified rewards, evaluators, interfaces, and safety constraints. Autonomous verification in the real world, and the evolution of the "embodied improver" itself, remain unsolved.

5. §4.3 RSI in software engineeringp.37

Why feedback is especially direct: Both the artifacts being developed and the coding agent itself can be represented as code, so both can be executed, versioned, and rolled back. Put simply, a bad change can be reverted with one click, and quality can be checked directly by running tests, conditions that are hard to find in other fields.

  • Repository tests are only an incomplete proxy and cannot fully capture user intent, safety, maintainability, or the quality of descendants;

Characteristics: RSI in medicine must be built on long-term, longitudinal feedback from expert opinion, clinical guidelines, diagnostic evidence, and patient outcomes.

§3.7 unifies the six levels through "handover of responsibility" and stresses that level and quality should be evaluated separately. The four domains in Chapter 4 each test this claim:

  • Outer components such as evaluators, acceptance criteria, and safety constraints are still generally set by humans;

§5.1 Theseus and §5.2 Feishu (Lark)

Key points of this part

  • Theseus Advocates "environment–data–model co-evolution": first improve the environment, then mine genuinely hard problems from it and generate training data to train the model; the stronger model then drives further iteration of the environment.   • Feishu (Lark) argues that for an agent to self-improve, it first needs a continuously updated data substrate. 5.1 Theseus: Core ideap.37

Theseus views "environment–data–model co-evolution" as the path toward next-generation recursive self-improvement (RSI) intelligence. Its basic logic consists of three loops:

  1. Environment makes knowledge obtainable and actions verifiable;
  2. Task execution produces evidence, and this evidence can be learned from;
  3. Improved model can explore a wider range of problems.

Under this vision, each round of work should leave reusable capabilities for later rounds, making real-world practice a source of continued growth in intelligencep.37.

In plain terms: it's not just "the model solves problems, gets them wrong, and gets retrained"; the "environment that sets the problems" gets improved too. The better the environment, the more it exposes what the model truly can't do; the stronger the model, the more it can help build harder environments and problems.

The four-step loop and Figure 10p.37–38

The paper turns this idea into four steps:

  1. Reconstruct Environment: turns past experience in "improving environments" into environment-refinement tasks, which are used to train an environment-refinement model that produces "task-ready" environments.
  2. Discover Hard Cases: The agent performs tasks in the reconstructed environment, identifies genuine capability gaps, and generates targeted training data accordingly.
  3. Train Task Model: Trains the task model on this hard-example data to improve its capabilities.
  4. Evolve Environment: Uses a stronger model to further iterate on the environment and generate more tasks.

Each subsequent round produces two things at once: new task training data and new experience in improving the environment. This ties "progress in problem-solving ability" to "progress in the ability to create conditions for future learning"p.38.

Figure 10 structure consists of two loops, one above the otherp.38:

  • Upper loop is "learning how to rebuild the environment". The Environment Refiner's role is to "learn how to reshape the environment".

5.2 Feishu (Lark): Building the data foundation for reliable enterprise-grade RSIp.39

Feishu approaches RSI from an often-overlooked prerequisite: for agents to improve themselves, they first need a continuously evolving data foundation.

In enterprise collaboration, documents, messages, meetings, and tasks constantly generate new information; agents consume this data while also producing new interaction traces. So Feishu treats three things as the foundation of its RSI pipeline:

  • Data production;
  • Quality evaluation;
  • Failure attribution.

The goal is not just to pile up data, but to ensure every iteration meets three conditionsp.39:

  1. there is fresh experience to learn from;
  2. There is a reliable criterion for judging progress;
  3. there is diagnosis fine-grained enough to determine what to change.

Figure 11 presents a five-step data-foundation loopp.39:

  1. Data collection: Comes from internal collaboration; heterogeneous data continuously generated in real work.
  2. Data organization: Extracts entities, events, and relations to build a time-driven context graph.
  3. Quality evaluation: Automated evaluation (absolute judgment + GSB) to identify errors and attribute their causes.
  4. Feedback construction: corrected data and standards flow back to earlier stages, usage traces add new evidence, and humans review the whole process.
  5. Improving agents in real work: Improves in-product task performance and user experience.

The output of these five steps then becomes the next round of "better data and evaluations."

Enterprise knowledge graphp.39

Feishu links information scattered across documents, messages, and meeting notes into entities, events, people, and temporal relationships, so agents can answer questions that require reasoning across sources.

Because errors in the graph propagate directly to downstream answers, graph construction itself is treated as a data-quality problem that requires repeated iteration. The approaches fall into two categories:

  • Scalability: Addressed with batched graph construction and delegated authorization checks;
  • Continuous verification:Continuously evaluates whether graph-based retrieval actually improves downstream QA.

Internal evaluation results, compared with a RAG-based baseline:

  • The human-rated task usability rate went from from 52% to 65%;
  • The usable rate of automated evaluation, from from 47% to 56%.

Automated data quality evaluationp.39

This system is the "measurement layer" for subsequent improvement. Its purpose is to stop relying entirely on expensive and inconsistent human review, and it works in two steps:

  1. First use Absolute judgment filter out clearly unusable outputs;
  2. Then use Comparative GSB evaluation Decides whether a new system variant should be promoted.

In plain terms: step one is the "passing bar", filtering out anything that doesn't qualify; step two is the "old vs. new comparison", where a new version must beat the old one to replace it.

The key design is Evaluation tied to explicit failure attribution. Bad cases are sorted into categories, such as temporal inconsistency, distorted queries, and poor source quality, so that each type of failure maps to a concrete direction for improvement. The paper gives two examples:

  • For time-sensitive questions, filter by the query time to avoid using "future information";
  • Low-quality synthetic queries are first rewritten, then fed back into the evaluation pipeline.

The initial automated evaluator agreed with human judgment about 84%. Most disagreements arise where, compared with human review, the automated evaluator is Stricter, which provides concrete signals for subsequent calibration.

  • Enterprise activity continuously produces new graph data and interaction traces;
  • Automated evaluation determines which outputs and data are reliable;

This loop turns production data from a "passive resource" into a "continuously evolving foundation for improvement"p.39–40.

§5.3 Xiaohongshu: Overall Approach — Recommendation Results Become "Signals for the Next Round of Updates"

p.40 This section is titled "Dual-Timescale RSI for Recommendation". Its subject is Xiaohongshu's Commercial recommender systems, whose core component is called Intent-Memory Agent (IMA).

A line at the top of Figure 12 states the core change: recommendation is no longer the end of the pipeline but "a signal for the next Memory update."

The traditional approach treats "understanding the user" as a one-time inference: review past behavior once, build a profile, then make recommendations. IMA instead chains the following five steps into a continuous loop:

  1. Feedback interpretation
  2. Memory update
  3. Experience retrieval
  4. Requirements reasoning
  5. Recommendation

After a recommendation is served, users' follow-up behavior includes clicks, dwell time, saves, searches, conversions, and negative feedback. These behaviors serve two purposes:

  • They are new behavioral inputs.
  • They are also Evidence check, used to check whether the system's earlier estimate of "user state and intent" was correct.

Based on these observations, the system performs five operations on memory:Add, reinforce, replace, decay, and Ignore Uninformative evidence.

So the authors say the target of this system's continual improvement is "the user representation that determines future recommendations", not just the output of the current recommendation.

In plain terms: each recommendation the system makes is "placing a bet". It watches whether the user's reaction confirms that judgment, then revises its understanding of that person accordingly.

Figure 12: Dual-loop structure

p.40 Figure 12 shows two loops.

(1) Inner loop: online user adaptation. The process is: user behavior → feedback understanding → memory update → experience retrieval → need inference → recommendation → online feedback, then back to the start. This loop runs fast and follows each individual user's real-time behavior.

(2) Outer loop: continuous system-level self-improvement. The process is: online feedback → Hard Case Mining → Judge and Verify → rule/data refinement → Post Training → new agent version, then redeployment.

The caption adds two points:

  • Hard cases go through Automated judging, human spot checks, and data reconstruction.
  • Post-training distills "verified experience" into the agent's parameters.

Structured memory and experience reuse

p.40 IMA splits intermediate state into three semantic layers:

  • Content: what the content topic is;
  • Who: What state the user is in;
  • Need: What the inferred needs are.

These three layers together determine how the knowledge base is stored and retrieved. When recalling past experience, it can be searched by "similar content," "similar user state," or "similar needs."

This split serves two purposes:

  • Reduce retrieval errors. Mixing different semantic factors into a single caption makes it easy to mismatch them during retrieval.
  • Targeted fixes. Later feedback can modify a specific state variable without rewriting the entire user profile.

In plain terms: instead of writing a vague "persona description" for a user, record it in three separate slots. Whichever slot is wrong, fix only that slot.

Fast adaptation and slow capability consolidation

p.40–41 The system combines two complementary improvement cycles.

User layer (fast). Real-time feedback updates memory to adapt to shifting interests and short-term needs. This is non-parametric adaptation; model weights are not changed.

System layer (slow). Collect high-value hard cases from online interactions, which then go through the following steps in order:

  1. Automated judging
  2. Human spot checks
  3. Rule correction
  4. Training data reconstruction

The resulting samples are used for post-training IMA, and the updated agent goes back into the recommendation pipeline.

The result of this two-layer division of labor:

  • Long-tail experience Can take effect immediately through the knowledge base;
  • Repeatedly validated patterns are then gradually consolidated into model parameters.

The authors summarize this design as connecting "fast non-parametric adaptation" with "more persistent model-level learning".

§5.4 Humanlaya: Background and Motivation

p.41 Humanlaya provides foundation model developers with Training and evaluation data. The production core is a complex Multi-file task package, a package typically includes:

  • Task description

In addition, task types and customer needs keep changing. Fixing failed samples one by one is not enough,The quality-control system itself must learn from recurring failures.

  • the data quality-check agent itself;
  • along with the accompanying rules, prompts, few-shot examples, and skills.

Figure 13: Delivery-driven dual loop

p.41 Figure 13 is titled "Learning from delivery feedback to improve the quality system."

(1) Inner loop: improve the current batch. The task package passes through three gates in sequence:

  1. Quality Checker: Checks content quality and consistency across components.
  2. Refiner: fixes confirmed defects.
  3. Delivery Checker: Checks format, structure, and delivery constraints.

If any step fails, the process enters a "locate → modify → recheck" loop, and delivery happens only after it passes.

(2) Outer loop: update the quality system. The process is:

  1. Feedback and reality: Collects human reviews, customer feedback, failure-fix records, and downstream usage.
  2. Analyze and propose updates: The update targets are rules, prompts, few-shot examples, and skills.
  3. Verification and human gatekeeping: Tested on held-out task bundles, then approved through human review.
  4. Release a new version v(t+1).

Data-centric inner loop

p.41 The inner loop runs only within the current delivery batch, with the goal of fixing the data currently being produced.

The paper gives an example: several that could have Independent scoring results were wrongly merged into a single coarse rubric item. The checker flags this rubric as "insufficiently atomic" and requires it to be split and restructured before accepting the task package.

The authors stress that this loop only improves the current artifact,does not by itself constitute lasting system improvement.

Improvement-centered outer loop (core RSI mechanism)

p.41–42 The outer loop runs across delivery batches; the authors call it the "core RSI mechanism". The steps are:

  1. After delivery, evidence is aggregated, including internal reviews, customer feedback, and downstream usage.
  2. An agent analyzes these cases and identifies Recurring root causes.
  3. Proposed for quality-control systems Versioned modifications, involving Refiner instructions, prompts, few-shot examples, and reusable skills.
  4. Candidate updates are placed in task bundles not involved in generating this modification for evaluation (i.e., held-out validation).
  5. It then goes through human review and is promoted only after passing.
  6. The new version replaces the old one for subsequent production batches.
  7. New tasks generate new evidence, which feeds into the next round of the loop.

In plain terms: the inner loop is "grading the homework", and the outer loop is "improving how the homework is graded".

Representative case: misjudged scanned PDFs

p.42 A three-page scanned PDF failed to parse in the extraction tool, so the quality checker marked the attachment as "unusable". Manual review found that the scan itself was perfectly clear. The root cause of the error was that the system treated "Extraction failed" and "Poor documentation quality" as the same thing.

The system then generated a new decision rule and accompanying few-shot examples to distinguish between the two cases. After held-out validation and human review, the update was merged into the next version and reused in subsequent tasks.

Based on this, the authors conclude that Humanlaya is a production-feedback-driven,Scaffold-level RSI. In other words, what gets improved is the rules and prompts around the model, not the model weights.

Quantitative results

p.42 Humanlaya reports an internal comparison: V0 versus the version that went through 4 feedback-driven updates the resulting V4.

The comparison conditions are:

  • the base model, tools and processing budget are all the same;
  • The evaluation set is 600 task packages not used in the update process.

Results:

  • The share of task packages that still contain critical defects after automated repair, from 9.0% down to 3.7%;
  • The average human handling time per task went from from 48 minutes down to 27 minutes.

Role of human-AI collaboration

p.42 The authors note that model capabilities are still improving rapidly, and agents still cannot complete some tasks fully on their own. So Humanlaya's approach is:

  • Agents carry out most of the self-improvement process;
  • Humans provide partial ground truth, review, and final approval at key points.

The authors view it as a practical example of "RSI + human oversight", rather than letting the self-improvement loop run unconstrained.

§5.5 ModelBest and §5.6 Tencent Hunyuan

Key points of this part

  • ModelBest takes the view that AI engineering will become autonomous sooner than open-ended AI research. It proposes "Forge Engineering", in which AI starts from an empty codebase and produces a production-ready implementation with no human involvement. The process is a two-level loop: the inner loop carries one scenario through to completion, and the outer loop writes the experience back into a knowledge base for the next scenario. 1. ModelBest's starting point: engineering is easier to automate first than researchp.42

ModelBest's entry point for advancing RSI is Automating AI engineering. The premise is that AI engineering is likely to become autonomous earlier than open-ended AI research, for two reasons:

  • Engineering tasks are labor-intensive, e.g., implementing training frameworks, optimizing kernels, configuring parallelism strategies, and running benchmarks;
  • But feedback for these tasks can be obtained directly by "execution", which is relatively cheap and objective. Whether code is correct and fast, you find out just by running it.

In plain terms: whether a research problem is "good" is hard to judge, but whether an engineering problem "passed, and how much faster it got" is clear as soon as you test it. That's why engineering is better suited to letting AI iterate in a closed loop on its own.

Based on this assessment, ModelBest proposes Forge Engineering, an end-to-end paradigm:

  • Input: Target model, hardware platform, parallelization requirements;
  • Process:An AI system starts from a an initially empty codebase and from there builds, tests, and refines the implementation on its own;
  • Goal: Produces production-ready implementations without human intervention.

2. Figure 14: The Two-Layer RSI Loopp.42

Figure 14 shows forging engineering as two nested loops, titled "Forge a scenario in the inner loop; write verified engineering experience back for future scenarios."

(1) Inner loop: forging a scenario

  2. System design + reference knowledge base: provides constraints and evaluation criteria;
  3. Deep optimization + Auto Research: generate implementations, measure, diagnose;
  4. Delivery: must pass both correctness and performance verification;
  5. If it fails, go back into the loop: "analyze → modify → re-measure".

(2) Outer loop: distilling experience across scenarios

  3. Update the knowledge base: maintain constraints and architectural lessons (the figure misspells Update as "Undate");

3. Inner layer: constrained architecture + autonomous low-level optimizationp.42–43

Within a single target scenario, the system combines two things:Constrained architecture design and Autonomous low-level optimization.

  • Knowledge base Maintained jointly by humans and AI, it contains specifications, evaluation criteria, and accumulated engineering experience.
  • The agent first uses this knowledge to determine a reasonable High-level architecture, while narrowing the search space.
  • Then we move to AutoResearch loop: Repeatedly generate implementations, measure correctness and performance, diagnose failures, and fix bottlenecks.
  • Successfully optimized parts are gradually merged into the main execution path; the operator examples cited are GEMM (general matrix multiplication) and FlashAttention.

The intent of this design is to keep global architectural constraints stable while allowing aggressive autonomous optimization at the implementation level.

In plain terms: people and the knowledge base lock in the house's frame first, and the AI is free to experiment with the interior within that frame, with the parts that turn out well getting fixed in place. This is fast and doesn't mess up the overall structure.

The engineering lessons from both successes and failures in each project are Write back to the shared knowledge base, specifically including:

  • Performance measurements;
  • Effective optimization strategies;
  • Reference implementations;
  • Failure rules, i.e., which approaches don't work.

These outputs from the previous project become the starting knowledge for the next one. As a result, the same engineering workflow can be reused across different models, hardware, and workloads.

ModelBest says it has already applied this paradigm in multiple areas: pre-training frameworks, operator libraries, reinforcement learning infrastructure, inference engines, fine-tuning, compression, quantization, and on-device deployment, across several hardware ecosystems.

Hyra extends the "experience-driven" approach to The evaluation itself.

Approach: uses accumulated search experience to revise the evaluation mechanism, then continues searching under the improved standard. Example revisions:

(1) Human-centric vs. agent-native research artifacts

The authors first recap the earlier discussion. Existing research has already automated five things to some degree:

  • Choose an improvement strategy;
  • Acquire experience;

Based on the autonomy levels, the four application domains, and industry practice, the authors propose the following 8 research directions.

Direction 1: Cross-component diagnosis and co-improvement (corresponding to L1→L2) p.46

Problem:Moving from L1 to L2 raises a diagnostic problem. Observing a failure often doesn't tell you which component to change. A wrong answer may stem from flawed source data, missing context, a faulty tool interface, an inadequate model, or a misjudgment by the evaluator.

  • Humanlaya case study (Section 5.4):An information extraction failure was initially attributed to poor document quality. In fact, what really needed changing was the quality checker's decision rules.
  • In science and embodied AI, the possible points of failure also include experimental protocols, perception, control, and environmental conditions.
  • When multiple components are adapting at the same time, changing one can break assumptions another component relies on.

Future practice:

  • Turns diagnostic conclusions into Testable intervention hypotheses, using controlled experiments to separately estimate the effect of individual changes and the interactions between changes;
  • Use techniques such as selectively freezing components, targeted ablations and explicitly recording component dependencies to distinguish a "real fix" from "merely compensating for an upstream defect";
  • Allocating search effort across data, models, the harness, and environments as constraints change.

In plain terms: don't just treat the symptoms. Run controlled experiments first to confirm which step the root cause is actually in.

  • Theseus case study (Section 5.1):its workspace experiments show that "environment improvements" and "changes to the task model" must be considered separately. However, it reports only component-level gains; a complete learning loop has not yet been demonstrated.

Research goals:understand which interventions improve the system as a whole and under what conditions, and whether this knowledge can guide subsequent improvement decisions.

Direction 2: Learner-conditioned experience acquisition and reliable learning signals (corresponding to L3) p.46–47

The L3 analysis distinguishes three properties of experience:

  1. whether Effective(whether the content is correct);
  2. For the current learner Difficulty How;
  3. whether learning it brings Lasting gains.

Two representative systems:

  • AZR: Uses executable checks to ensure validity, while generating problems based on the learner's current state;
  • R-Zero: Uses the consistency of solver answers as a proxy for difficulty.

The authors' assessment is:Neither "correctness" nor "difficulty estimate" alone proves that a problem has learning value. An adaptive curriculum can run into the following problems:

  • Repeatedly selecting ambiguous problems;
  • reinforce the evaluator's own errors;
  • cluster in a narrow region where "progress is easy to measure".

These errors enter both the learner and the state that "decides the next batch of experience," creating a vicious cycle.p.47

Future practice:

  • Estimate learning value across multiple rounds while keeping experience valid and diverse, and covering already-learned capabilities (to prevent forgetting);
  • Sources of experience include selecting from existing data, generating new tasks, actively seeking interaction, and requesting stronger external feedback when internal judgment is insufficient;
  • There are two core problems: When is expensive verification worth doing? How should uncertain experience influence subsequent training?

Evaluation design recommendations: different methods may acquire different data distributions, but data-source access and the total "acquisition + learning" budget must be matched. Two controls can also test whether "learner conditioning" itself yields transfer gains beyond "just training more":

  • Freeze the learner state fed into the acquisition mechanism;
  • Switch to a fixed curriculum that is independent of the learner.

Direction 3: Persistent state management and reliable reuse (corresponding to L4) p.47

L4 systems inherit parameters, memory, skills, tools, and harness code. But "retaining an artifact" is not the same as "later agents actually benefiting from it." Failures fall into two categories:

  • Activation failed: the relevant skills were never retrieved;
  • Execution failure: The skill was retrieved but not executed correctly.

Accumulation itself also causes problems:

  • Library Drift study shows that an ever-growing skill library degrades retrieval and stalls improvement;
  • But retiring old skills too aggressively is equally harmful.

So the key to persistence is ongoing management of whether these artifacts still apply and how they interact with one another.

In plain terms: a skill library is like a company knowledge base. Storing more doesn't make it more useful; someone has to regularly organize, verify, and retire entries.

Future practice:

  • attach to each inherited artifact its supporting evidence, applicability conditions, dependencies, and its actual effect on subsequent tasks;
  • HDSO paired evaluation can serve as a starting point for an "admission test." But when the task distribution, executor, or surrounding systems change, verification must continue;
  • Measure four things separately: update quality, activation, faithful execution, and downstream benefit; then study when an artifact should be merged, revised, retired, or revalidated;
  • Cross-model reuse is a separate issue: skills that help the author model may not work for a different executor.

Solving these problems is what connects "successfully generating updates" with "capabilities carrying over reliably across sessions and generations."

Direction 4: Controlled adaptation under domain-specific feedback p.47

Applying the same improvement loop to four domains requires different verification strategies:

  • Science:experimental failures may stem from hypotheses, experimental protocols, or instruments; evidence must retain its underlying assumptions and uncertainties.
  • Embodied AI:the current policy determines which states can be observed; physical trials consume resources and may have irreversible consequences.
  • Software: Has executable feedback, but "tests passing" still depends on the requirements specification and test coverage.
  • Healthcare: Outcome feedback is delayed and heavily confounded; an update validated in one population or institution may not transfer elsewhere.

These differences constrain two things at once: what a system can learn autonomously, and what kind of evidence is sufficient to support deployment.

Future practice:

  • When formulating update policies, distinguish occasional failures from recurring capability limitations, and choose the appropriate validation process for each type of change;
  • first use simulation, historical replay, and sandboxed execution for initial screening, then run supervised or staged evaluation under the target operating conditions;
  • Continuous monitoring: whether gains hold after requirements, populations, tools, or environments change;
  • Software failures can be rolled back to a checkpoint, but physical-world and clinical consequences cannot be undone, so in these settings,Pre-release validation is essential;
  • For high-stakes releases, access permissions, and safety constraints, human decision authority must remain explicit.

Open questions:how to increase useful adaptations while limiting the propagation of errors into future interactions.

Direction 5: Trustworthy evolution of improvement mechanisms (corresponding to L5) p.47–48

At L5, the object being modified becomes the improver, evaluator, or research strategy itself, which changes how subsequent successors "are produced and accepted". This gives rise to a The coupled verification problem:

  • A stronger solver may expose weaknesses in the evaluator;
  • But changing the evaluator can make old and new scores incomparable, or let the system game the evaluator (reward exploitation).

Two related systems:

  • RQGM addresses part of this: freezing the evaluator within an epoch, validating new evaluators against an independent "anchor", and retroactively re-scoring results affected by evaluator replacement.
  • Hyra The evaluator improvements in this work raise the same larger question: as search keeps advancing, how can adaptive metrics remain trustworthy?

In plain terms: if the "test-taker" and the "examiner and grader" evolve together, you must keep an external ruler that doesn't change along with them.

Future practice:

  • Separate "editable internal feedback mechanisms" from "independently maintained acceptance criteria";
  • Preserve an evidence chain linking each mechanism change to subsequent decisions;
  • first evaluate the effects of changes to "candidate generation" and to "evaluation" separately, then attribute the gains to their co-evolution;p.48
  • A-Evolve-Training demonstrates strategy inheritance under a fixed high-level goal, but whether these strategies transfer beyond the research settings they evolved in has yet to be tested.

Open questions:

  • How can evaluation stay independent when it is accessed adaptively and repeatedly?
  • How can collusion-like correlated errors between the proposer and the evaluator be detected?
  • How can harmful mechanism changes be reverted without losing useful experience?

The authors stress that adjusting research priorities at the operational level,Not necessary, and should not Also hands over the decision on the system's overall mission.

Direction 6: Long-horizon evaluation of inherited improvement capabilities p.48

The key here is distinguishing two kinds of L5:

  • Structural L5: can show that the modified mechanism is inherited and actually invoked;
  • Effective L5:it must be shown that the mechanism produces or selects better subsequent improvements.

The latter is currently a substantial empirical gap. Two examples:

  • reviewed in this survey AIDE2 In experiments, when the evolved outer scaffold was used as the outer-loop improver,Did not achieved a statistically significant efficiency advantage;
  • Research on software lineages suggests separating "current task performance" from "the ability to produce useful offspring," because temporarily weaker variants may enable later progress.

Evaluation recommendations:

  • Start from comparable initial systems and evidence, and compare the original mechanism with the modified one under a matched total budget. The total budget must include the cost of developing and evaluating these mechanisms themselves;
  • Freeze the evolved mechanism and test it on new tasks to see whether its improvement strategy transfers,HyperAgents does exactly this;
  • Long-term studies should report full trajectories across multiple repeated runs, including rejected updates, performance regressions, recovery, retained capabilities and resource consumption;
  • Protected reference tasks keep results comparable, while new tasks and unfamiliar constraints test transfer and discovery.

These evaluation protocols ultimately aim to answer three questions: Do inherited changes alter the capacity for subsequent improvement? When do gains plateau? Does the apparent "acceleration" still hold once the extra search and evaluation costs are accounted for?

Direction 7: Resource-aware improvement and human-AI collaboration p.48

RSI starts from the goal of improving efficiency, so it must account for The entire improvement process cost. Faster kernels, better training recipes, and longer unattended runs may still rely on large amounts of candidate generation, evaluation, infrastructure maintenance, and human review.

  • Meta regression analysis workflow, retaining engineer review of system-proposed changes;
  • Humanlaya Keep human verification, and report processing time alongside quality.

This shows that when evaluating autonomy, you must look at both how much human effort is needed and what type of human effort is needed. In addition, stronger base models and more parallel trials can also confound comparisons between different improvement strategies.

Future practice:

  • Study how to allocate the total budget across diagnosis, experience acquisition, candidate search, validation, and deployment monitoring;
  • Cheap initial screening can reduce expensive trials, provided its predictions are continually calibrated against independent outcomes;
  • Reusing verified tools and past failure records can reduce duplicated work, but their maintenance cost must also be counted;
  • Evaluations should report the time and total cost needed to "reach a verified capability target", as well as review effort and the amount of rework;
  • Adaptive stopping is a research focus: the system must use expected learning value and uncertainty to justify that "further experiments are worthwhile", and explicitly define conditions for pausing, escalating to humans, and terminating unproductive searches.

Direction 8: Reproducible infrastructure supporting cross-round inheritance p.48–49

A successor system needs to know an improvement how it was obtained, including failed alternatives and the conditions under which the evidence holds. The four industrial cases each reflect part of these requirements:

  • Lark:emphasizes time-anchored data and failure attribution;
  • Humanlaya: versioned updates to the retained quality system;
  • Hyra:stores executable experience;
  • Agent-Native Research Lab: Proposes a form of research artifact that preserves code, specifications, exploration history, and empirical traces.

These practices show the need for shared infrastructure that carries research evidence forward from one round to the next. How much this inheritance pays off in the long run, however, remains to be shown.

Design recommendations:

  • A reusable artifact format should capture the full chain: parent state → proposed change → evidence driving the change → evaluation configuration → acceptance decision → subsequent usage;
  • Version data, environments, and evaluators so runs can be replayed and it is clear which comparisons remain valid after an update;
  • Deterministic re-extraction can check the measurements in a report, and formal verification can prove that a system satisfies certain properties with respect to a specification. But neither can prove that the experiment or the specification itself truly captures the intended capability;
  • Public evaluations should combine inspectable artifacts with replay studies, and Reported separately Source of results, artifact availability, and independent replication status;
  • When data is confidential, you can provide a shareable evaluation subset and a controlled replay interface for partial verification, and clearly state what cannot be verified.p.49

Chapter 6 wrap-up: what counts as true RSI p.49

The authors set out the criterion for the whole chapter: progress toward true RSI must show up as the "ability to improve" itself being recurring, attributable, and transferable improvement. The paper calls this "repeated, attributable, and transferable improvements in the capacity to improve".

The key evidence is that, under explicit resource and permission constraints, inherited changes make later rounds perform better in one of the following three ways:

  • Acquire more useful experience;
  • Discover better interventions;
  • Verify successors more effectively.

In plain terms: "this generation got stronger" isn't enough. You have to show that "this generation makes the next generation better at getting stronger".

  3. Connecting the four application domains: Scientific discovery, embodied intelligence, software engineering, and healthcare. Whether feedback is available, how costly verification is, and what deployment constraints apply together determine how far each field has progressed toward RSI.

  • 3.6 Harness Implementation / Scaffold Code:source code implementing agent scaffolding, orchestration logic, workflow controllers, and runtime coordination mechanisms. Put simply, the first 5 items change the "design," while this one directly changes "the code that implements the design."

4. Tools & Skillsp.64

5 sub-goals in total:

  • 4.1 Tool Set / Inventory: The tools, APIs, external services, and capabilities the system can call.
  • 4.2 Tool Definition / Interface:tool descriptions, function signatures, schemas, API wrappers, and input/output specifications.
  • 4.3 Tool Implementation:the executable code and logic inside tools.
  • 4.4 Skill / Macro Library: Reusable skills, macros, subroutines, procedures, and higher-level behavior modules.
  • 4.5 Skill Composition: rules and structures for assembling low-level skills into higher-level workflows or capabilities.

This category builds up layer by layer: 4.1 covers "what tools exist," 4.2 "how tools are described," 4.3 "how tools are implemented internally," and 4.4–4.5 cover accumulating and composing skills on top of tools.

5. Modelp.64

4 sub-goals in total:

  • 5.1 Model Weights:Trainable parameters of the backbone model.
  • 5.2 Adapter / Auxiliary Module:Trainable parameters of add-on components such as LoRA, adapters, and memory modules.
  • 5.3 Architecture: Network structure, module composition, connectivity, and computational structure.
  • 5.4 Inference Policy / Configuration: Persistent decoding strategies, test-time strategies, inference configurations, and runtime settings. The original uses "persistent" to emphasize that these changes are locked in, not temporary adjustments made for a single call.

6. Trainer / Optimization Systemp.65

This category modifies the "training machinery that improves the model" itself. It has 5 sub-goals:

  • 6.1 Training Objective: Loss functions, training rewards, learning objectives, and optimization criteria.
  • 6.2 Optimizer / Update Rule: optimizers, parameter-update algorithms, learning-rate schedules, and related hyperparameters.
  • 6.3 Training Schedule / Pipeline:the ordering, configuration, and coordination of training stages such as fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and distillation.
  • 6.4 Data Selection / Curriculum: Rules for selecting training samples, data mixing ratios, difficulty progression, and curriculum strategies.
  • 6.5 Search / Meta-optimization Procedure: Evolutionary, search, selection, and meta-optimization processes used to generate and select candidate improvements.

7. Evaluator & Feedback Systemp.65

4 sub-goals in total:

  • 7.1 Evaluator / Judge: Evaluators, critics, scorers, and judge models that assess candidate outputs or system variants.
  • 7.2 Reward / Fitness Function:reward models, fitness functions, utility functions, preference models, and scoring rules.
  • 7.3 Verifier: Correctness verifiers, test generators, proof checkers, consistency checks, and other validation mechanisms.
  • 7.4 Feedback / Credit Assignment: the mechanism that converts observed outcomes into improvement signals. Signals can be local, aggregate, or assigned over time. Put simply, it answers the question: "When a task goes well or badly, which step deserves the credit or blame?"

8. Data & Environmentp.65

4 sub-goals in total:

  • 8.1 Training / Experience Data: Training sets, replay buffers, experience pools, interaction trajectories, and other data used for later learning.
  • 8.2 Task / Curriculum Generator:The component that generates tasks, problems, challenges, or training episodes for the next round of improvement.
  • 8.3 Environment / Simulator: Environment dynamics, simulators, interaction rules, task worlds, and the structure of how the system interacts with its environment.
  • 8.4 World Model:learned models that represent, predict, generate, or simulate environment states and dynamics.

9. External Artifactp.65

This category of improvement targets not the system itself, but what the system produces. It has 3 sub-goals:

  • 9.1 Program / Solution Code: Task-level programs, patches, or solution code produced by the system. The original text specifically notes that here Does not include the code that implements the agent itself; that part belongs to 3.6. This is a key boundary: modifying its own code counts as framework-level improvement, while modifying task code counts only as external output.
  • 9.2 Algorithm: Algorithms, compute kernels, mathematical procedures, symbolic methods, and computational techniques.
  • 9.3 Scientific / Design Artifact: Research outputs such as scientific hypotheses, experimental designs, architectures, circuits, and engineering designs.

10. Full-system / Co-evolutionp.65

This category handles cases where "several things are changed at once." It has 3 sub-goals:

  • 10.1 Multi-target Harness: Modifies multiple harness-level components, such as prompts, memory, workflows, and tools, within the same improvement process.
  • 10.2 Model–Harness Co-evolution: Over multiple rounds of improvement, both the model parameters and the surrounding harness/scaffold are modified.
  • 10.3 Improvement-loop / Meta-RSI: The mechanism itself that generates, evaluates, selects, and applies candidate changes across successive rounds of improvement. In plain terms, this is "improving 'the way improvement is done.'"

10.3 and 6.5 differ in what they target. 6.5 targets the search process of "generating and selecting candidates" in training optimization. 10.3 targets the overall loop that spans the entire multi-round self-improvement process.

Appendix C: Details of the four application domains

Key points of this part

  • Appendix C examines Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) in four application domains: scientific research (S1), embodied intelligence (S2), software engineering (S3) and healthcare (S4). Each domain follows the same structure: first the typical workflow and three main challenges in the field, then representative systems grouped by "which component is evolving," and finally an assessment using the B0–L5 levels of how far they are from "true RSI."
  • The four domains reach highly consistent conclusions: B0 (within-task output correction) is already widespread; L1 (persistent updates) has been shown to be feasible;L2 is currently the most solid frontier, i.e., the system can autonomously select and apply updates, but the objectives and evaluators are fixed externally; L3 and L4 have only sporadic or simulation-only examples; L5 (evolution of the improver itself) is essentially unrealized, at most a “bounded L5”.
  • The authors repeatedly stress that whether evolution is real depends on whether improvements can be verifiably reused on later, new tasks, not on how much the tool library or memory store has grown. Before an improvement is inherited, it needs provenance records, a defined scope of applicability, uncertainty estimates, version control, and rollback mechanisms.

1. Overall framework of Appendix Cp.72

The main text already grades systems from B0 to L5 by "which improvement functions are handed over to AI control." Appendix C takes a different angle and looks at how RSI is applied in different settings. The authors point out (alongside Fig. 9) that the settings differ mainly in two respects:

  • The object of improvement differ;
  • Verify and retain the feedback needed for an update differ.

The four scenarios are not mutually exclusive. For example, a "medical AI scientist" can belong to both S1 and S4.

Each subsection follows the same three-step structure:

  1. first describe the AI workflow in the scenario, explain how the RSI loop arises from the tasks and the available feedback, and cover the challenges specific to the scenario;
  2. Then group representative systems by "which component or process they improve", and compare where they intervene in the loop, what they update, and how they retain improvements;
  3. Finally, we assess the gap between existing research and a complete RSI loop, including the strength of the evidence, scenario-specific risks, and the main obstacles.

2. S1: RSI in Scientific Researchp.72–74

Why scientific settings are specialp.72

The authors consider science one of the most consequential settings for RSI. This is because scientific progress depends not only on solving isolated problems but also on improving the mechanisms for "how future questions, hypotheses, and experiments are formulated."

So scientific RSI must be distinguished from ordinary automated discovery. Merely finding a better molecule, equation, or hypothesis does not count as scientific RSI. The scientific RSI loop works as follows:

  1. Generate candidate hypotheses and experiments from research challenges;
  2. Evaluate via computation or physical experiments;
  3. Use the resulting evidence or falsifications to diagnose the AI research system (AI4Sci) itself.

Three major challenges:

  • Open-endedness (open-ended): The space of relevant hypotheses, the tools needed, and the experiments are all unknown in advance. Experiments are also expensive, so "how to explore" itself becomes part of the improvement problem.
  • Feedback is sparse, delayed, and non-identifying:A failed experiment may mean the hypothesis was wrong, or the model was unsuitable, the experimental protocol invalid, or the instruments unreliable. Put simply: you only know “it failed,” not what to blame.
  • Effectiveness is conditional:A conclusion depends on assumptions, domain, and measurement process. An update that succeeds in one place cannot be safely inherited unless its provenance, scope, and uncertainty are recorded.

The authors therefore frame scientific RSI as a threefold problem: evidence acquisition, capability evolution, and epistemic control. Existing work is organized around three evolvable components: the hypothesis module, the experiment agent, and the reflection system/improver.

C.1.1 Evolving the “hypothesis module”p.72

The hypothesis module decides which explanations or candidate methods to propose, and which to try first. Real evolution requires that feedback changes the mechanism for generating future hypotheses, rather than just picking a better hypothesis for the current problem.

The first approach is to accumulate experience and memory:

  • HypoForge: continually distills research experience into reusable "procedural skills" covering hypothesis generation, experimental design, and execution. Without direct experimental feedback, it updates generation skills using critiques from a discriminator; testing skills are revised based on actual execution results and failure attribution.
  • EvoScientist: Maintains an "ideation memory" that records promising and failed research directions, which later tasks retrieve to adjust their proposals. This is persistent, memory-based adaptation, but the underlying model, memory structure, and update process are all fixed.

The second approach is more direct: it modifies the candidate generator itself:

  • TTT-Discover: Applies reinforcement learning at test time, letting scientific feedback directly update model weights while solving new problems.
  • A self-improving framework for polymer discovery: adds simulation-evaluated candidates to the training data and retrains the generative model.

The authors' assessment: The evolution in these systems is task- or domain-specific and arises from fixed training and selection pipelines. Current evidence supports only “bounded improvement at the hypothesis level”; no general mechanism has yet emerged that gets increasingly better at proposing scientific hypotheses across domains.

C.1.2 Evolving the “experiment agent”p.73

The experiment agent turns hypotheses into executable steps, including choosing tools, writing code, and operating lab equipment. It evolves when execution feedback is turned into reusable actions.

(a) Evolve tools and skills so the action space can expand:

  • Test-Time Tool Evolution: Synthesizes, verifies, and reuses scientific tools at inference time. Its SciEvo benchmark contains 1,590 tasks, by 925 evolved tools support.
  • CASCADE: Creates, repairs, and accumulates executable skills for chemistry and materials science.
  • S1-NexusAgent: distills complete research trajectories into reusable "scientific skills."
  • SkillFoundry:Mines skills from heterogeneous sources such as papers, code repositories, documentation, and APIs, compiles them into executable skill packages, then validates, merges, or prunes them based on execution results and downstream utility, continuously maintaining the skill library.

These methods make the agent's action space persistent and extensible. The limitation is that tool generation and admission rules are still externally specified.

(b) Evolve broader experimental strategies and workflows:

  • DrugSAGE: Stores validated modeling workflows, training strategies, and fixes for recurring errors in cross-task memory. From 16 tasks The accumulated experience improved 17 held-out tasks performance. The authors see this as direct evidence that “inherited experience really does change behavior on new problems.”
  • STELLA: For biomedical research, it updates reasoning templates and expands a tool library called Tool Ocean.
  • EarthLink: For climate science, it keeps successful scripts and analysis workflows.
  • OriGene: uses human and experimental feedback to revise thinking templates, tool combinations, and analysis protocols.
  • Self-evolving fluid control agent: Modifies both the "agent definition" and the white-box controllers it produces in physics simulation.

The fluid control example also reveals a Evaluation boundaries: Improvement of the agent itself must be measured separately from improvement of the scientific output it produces (here, the controller). Put simply: a better controller doesn't necessarily mean the "controller builder" has gotten better.

Section summary: The strongest evidence comes from verified reuse on later tasks, not from growth in the size of the tool library or memory store. For experimental agents to evolve reliably, they need explicit skill preconditions, execution tests, provenance records, versioning, and rollback. Otherwise, a workflow that has only succeeded locally may be applied under conditions where it was never validated.

C.1.3 Evolving the “reflection system and improver”p.73

First, distinguish two roles:

  • Reflection system: interpret evidence and identify the causes of failure;
  • Improver: decides which component to change, how to change it, and whether to keep the change.

A fixed critic that only corrects the current output executes a predefined improvement procedure. An "evolving improver", by contrast, updates its own diagnosis, intervention, or promotion strategy based on the results of past improvements.

Representative systems:

  • SIA: Among existing AI4Sci systems, it has the broadest component-level update loop. Its FeedbackAgent chooses among several kinds of modifications based on task results: changing the agent scaffold, changing tools, changing retry and search logic, or changing LoRA (low-rank adapter) weights using different strategies.
  • CORAL: Replaces fixed evolutionary search heuristics with long-running asynchronous agents. These agents autonomously review past attempts, decide when to test and when to call the evaluator, and externalize their findings as shared attempt records, notes, and reusable skills. It also uses a "heartbeat" to trigger reflection and course correction, sustaining long-horizon exploration.
  • SAGA: extends the editable scope to Goal layer. Under a fixed high-level scientific goal, it diagnoses failure modes across the population of optimized candidates, proposes or reweights concrete sub-goals, and implements those sub-goals as executable scoring functions that guide subsequent search.

C.1.4 Toward recursively improving scientific agentsp.73–74

The ultimate form of scientific RSI is a Increasingly capable of original science system, not just one that produces better hypotheses or results. It must improve hypothesis generation, experiment execution, and evidence interpretation together, so that each research round yields both scientific findings and reusable improvements for future discoveries.

Current status by level:

  • B0: Output correction is already common.
  • L1: Persistent updates have been achieved through model retraining and memory accumulation; examples include EvoScientist, TTT-Discover, and the polymer framework.
  • L2: The strongest current frontier. Systems can autonomously select or apply updates to weights, tools, skills, workflows, and scaffolds. Examples include Test-Time Tool Evolution, SIA, and CASCADE.
  • L3:a few systems show some of these characteristics through adaptive exploration and cross-task experience reuse, e.g., DrugSAGE and CORAL.
  • L4, L5: End-to-end environment adaptation (L4) and evolution of the improver itself (L5) have barely been explored.

How far are we from true scientific RSI? This requires three things: more reliable attribution of experimental results, more selective transfer of improvements across tasks, and stronger evaluation of whether successive updates actually make future research better. Equally important, as increasingly capable components of the improvement process become adaptive, provenance records, reproducibility, and stable scientific and safety constraints must be preserved.

3. S2: RSI in embodied intelligencep.74–76

Scenario characteristics and three main challengesp.74

In embodied AI, an agent's actions produce observable consequences in physical or simulated environments, making it a natural fit for RSI. But there are three major challenges:

  • Experience is endogenous:the current policy determines which states the agent can reach, and therefore what evidence it can obtain for further improvement. Put simply: you can only learn from “places you can reach.”
  • Capability is distributed across the entire perception–motor system:perception, planning, control, and even the body itself are all involved, making it hard to attribute failures to any single component.
  • Physical trials cannot be freely replayed, undone, or reset: candidate updates must be evaluated under safety, hardware, and resource constraints.

Embodied RSI therefore has to address three problems at once: experience generation, credit assignment across components, and safe inheritance.

The interaction loop the authors describe is:

  1. Environments and curricula provide learning tasks;
  2. the agent harness organizes the relevant capabilities;
  3. the policy executes actions;
  4. The world model and evaluator interpret the results;
  5. Verified feedback is written back to each component and fed into the next round.

The next four subsections map directly to these four types of components.

C.2.1 Evolving environments and curriculap.74

The first step of the loop is deciding what the agent experiences and what it learns from it.

Adjusting tasks within existing simulators:

  • POET: Replaces hand-designed fixed curricula by co-evolving a population of "environment–agent pairs." It mutates environments, uses a "minimal criterion" to accept only challenges that are neither too easy nor unsolvable, optimizes the corresponding policies, and periodically transfers policies to other environments to reuse behavioral "stepping stones."
  • EnvGen: Deploying large language models (LLMs) directly as embodied agents is costly, and static curricula can't target individual weaknesses. Its approach is to have the LLM act as a curriculum designer: the LLM generates simulator configurations, a smaller reinforcement learning (RL) agent trains in them, and skill-level performance is fed back to the LLM, which generates environments that specifically target the remaining weaknesses.
  • GenEnv: Addresses the mismatch where "static training data can't keep up with an increasingly capable learner" by parameterizing the environment simulator as a trainable curriculum policy. It uses an α-Curriculum Reward to favor tasks near the edge of the learner's current ability; the learner is optimized with GRPO, while the simulator is updated via reward-weighted regression.

Breaking out of the predefined environment space:

  • OMNI-EPIC: Addresses how hand-predefined environment distributions limit the search space by using an LLM to directly generate executable environment code and reward code. Later proposals draw on an archive of past tasks and the current agent's learning progress to find challenges that are both learnable and novel.
  • SimWorld Studio: Addresses the problem that generated 3D content is often static, unverifiable, and unsuitable for embodied training. Its SimCoder agent writes executable Unreal Engine code, revises environments based on compiler errors, physics checks, and vision-language model (VLM) critiques, and saves successful tools and skills for later use. The learner's performance then guides it to generate increasingly difficult, Gym-compatible worlds.

Section summary: These methods turn the learner's performance into a mechanism for evolving “the distribution of future embodied experience and its generator.”

C.2.2 Evolving skills, memory, and the harnessp.75

The harness determines how memory, skills, tools and execution rules are assembled into a system that can act.

Skill level:

  • Voyager: writes successful behaviors in Minecraft into an executable skill library.
  • LRLL: Expands and reorganizes a library of composable robot programs through self-guided exploration and a "wake–sleep" process.
  • EmbodiSkill: distinguishes two kinds of failure: the skill itself is wrong, or the agent did not follow valid guidance. It updates a skill only when the trajectory provides relevant evidence.

At the architecture level, the update target expands from a single skill to the entire surrounding architecture:

  • SHAPER: Co-evolves reusable skills and a "context–code" framework through environment rollouts.
  • ASPIRE: Diagnosing robot execution failures, verifying candidate fixes, and keeping successful fixes as transferable skills.
  • ENPIRE: Going further, coding agents are allowed to modify robot policies, training pipelines, and supporting infrastructure through repeated real-world experiments.

Section summary: These systems treat evolution as a system-level matter. Experience changes not only what the agent “knows” but also how its capabilities “are selected, composed, and improved.” The resulting harness determines how the policy turns observations and instructions into actions.

C.2.3 Evolving policies and action modelsp.75

Overall improvement strategy:

  • Self-Improving Embodied Foundation Models:Derives reward and success signals from pretrained models, letting robots practice tasks and improve policies with limited human supervision.
  • MEDAL++: Learns to both "complete a task" and "undo a task," reducing the need for humans to reset the environment and enabling autonomous practice.

Change only more targeted components:

  • Q-Planning: Keeps a large behavior-cloning policy fixed and continuously trains a smaller value function on successful and failed deployment trajectories.
  • SERP: Adjusts the action model based on recent navigation failures before replanning.

What these methods have in common is that interactions produced by the current policy become training evidence for the next generation of policies.

Limitations: The update algorithm, the reward definition, and the model components that may be modified are all fixed in advance, so these methods mainly count as “policy-level self-improvement”. In addition, for experience to be inherited safely, the system must first be able to predict and evaluate the actual consequences of its own actions. This leads to the next section.

C.2.4 Evolving world models and evaluatorsp.75

The world model and evaluator turn interaction results into predictions, outcome judgments, and learning signals.

  • VLAW: Uses real-robot rollouts to improve an action-conditioned video world model, then uses that model to generate synthetic experience for training vision–language–action (VLA) policies.
  • World-VLA-Loop: turns the relationship above into an iterative loop. Policy failures are used to refine the world model, and the refined model in turn provides a more reliable environment for the next round of policy optimization.
  • Motus2: integrates policy, simulation, and evaluation into one shared model, using failed and suboptimal interactions to improve dynamics prediction and value prediction.
  • SAIL: A video planner executes plans it generated itself, then fine-tunes itself on the resulting trajectories.

Section summary: These methods improve not only the policy but also the mechanisms that “predict consequences and generate future training signals.” Their feedback can update the next round's policy, harness, or curriculum, closing the embodied RSI loop. But the objectives and update procedures remain externally fixed, so this is “bounded model–policy co-evolution,” not unbounded recursive improvement.

C.2.5 Toward recursively improving embodied agentsp.75–76

The ideal division of labor in an embodied loop is as follows:

  • Environment generators propose tasks near the capability frontier;
  • the harness assembles the relevant memory, skills, tools and model;
  • the policy interacts with the environment;
  • The world model and evaluator interpret the results;
  • Verified improvements are inherited by the policy, world model, or framework.

In this way, each round of interaction improves both current behavior and future learning.

Current status by level:

  • B0: Reflection and replanning within an episode are common.
  • L1:policy and model updates based on embodied experience, e.g., Self-Improving Embodied Foundation Models, MEDAL++, and SAIL.
  • L2:The main frontier. Under a fixed evaluation pipeline, select and apply updates to skills, prompts, control programs, and harnesses; examples include SHAPER, EmbodiSkill, and ASPIRE.
  • L3: Generates experience based on the learner's state and retains validated skills across tasks; examples include Voyager and LRLL.
  • L4: Agent–environment co-evolution has emerged, but mainly in simulation; examples include POET, EnvGen, GenEnv, and SimWorld Studio.
  • Bounded L5:ENPIRE lets coding agents modify policies, training pipelines, and infrastructure, which comes close to this level. However, its environment interfaces, evaluators, and safety constraints are still externally specified.

How far are we from true embodied RSI? Agents need to actively acquire informative experience, attribute outcomes across the entire sensorimotor system, and verify updates under physical conditions where resets are not freely available. Improvements must remain effective across tasks, environments, and embodiments, with explicit provenance, uncertainty, and rollback. The system must also learn to improve its own harness and evaluation pipeline without weakening fixed safety constraints. Each of these capabilities has been demonstrated separately, but they have not yet been integrated into a safe, persistent, autonomous real-world improvement loop.

4. S3: RSI in software engineeringp.76–78

Scenario characteristics and three main challengesp.76

Software engineering is the most concrete setting for studying RSI because Both the product and the agents that develop it are executable code. The typical loop is:

  1. A coding agent attempts to resolve an issue;
  2. Observe feedback from the code repository, such as test failures, execution traces, and code reviews;
  3. Use this evidence to modify some persistent part of itself, such as skills, workflows, or implementation code;
  4. Accepted modifications are reused in subsequent tasks.

Three major challenges:

  • Hard to design tasks: Software tasks targeting current capability gaps must be generated continuously, and these tasks need to be both verifiable and valuable for future learning.
  • Evaluation should look at two levels:whether a modification works now, and whether it improves the “ability to keep improving.” A modification may immediately raise benchmark scores yet stall later search; conversely, a temporarily weaker variant may produce stronger descendants.
  • Multi-component co-evolution:when multiple components evolve at the same time, the outer contract, such as evaluation criteria and improvement goals, must still be upheld.

Existing work is organized around three evolving components: agent implementation and surrounding harness, development experience and collaboration, and the improvement process itself.

C.3.1 Evolving the implementation and harness of coding agentsp.76–77

The most direct approach is to treat the coding agent itself as an editable software project.

  • SICA: The agent can view previous versions and benchmark results, modify its own Python codebase, and use the selected version in the next round. Because the same system is both the developer and the thing being developed, changes to file-editing tools, sub-agents, or context management can improve both general coding and subsequent self-modification. However, its benchmarks, utility function, and sandbox are fixed externally.

More recent work narrows the scope of self-modification to the surrounding scaffold, including middleware, verification rules, and control logic:

  • Self-Harness: Uses execution traces to identify recurring weaknesses, has the same underlying model propose small changes to the current framework, and keeps a change only if it passes regression testing.
  • Agentic Harness Engineering: exposes each harness component as a separately editable, revertible file, distills long trajectories into structured evidence, and checks whether each change produced the effect it predicted. It has a separate "evolver" role, so it is less directly self-referential than Self-Harness, but accepted harnesses likewise persist into later runs.
  • Ouroboros: extends this idea to deployment. Changes to tools, context assembly, prompts, and the core implementation, once reviewed, directly become the runtime used for subsequent work.

Section summary: Version control, regression testing, and rollback can turn an agent's self-modification into a persistent, auditable process.

C.3.2 Evolving development experience, skills, and collaborationp.77

A lighter form of self-evolution doesn't rewrite the full implementation. It only changes how the agent uses past development experience.

  • SWE-Exp: extracts both successful and failed issue-resolution trajectories into an experience library and retrieves relevant lessons in later repository tasks.
  • CODESKILL:Rather than just storing cases, it learns a management policy that extracts procedural skills at multiple levels, updates or deletes skills as new trajectories arrive, and maintains a compact skill library.

The persistent output of both is reusable development knowledge that supports cross-task adaptation. But their extraction and update processes are fixed and do not improve with use.

At the team organization level:

  • EvoMAC: represents a multi-agent coding workflow as a network of role prompts plus communication links, and uses test feedback and textual back-propagation to modify both the agents and the connections between them. This improves the workflow that builds the current program, but the reported updates occur only at test time for each task.

C.3.3 Evolving the improvement process itselfp.77

The strongest systems do not just advance along a single sequence of “accepted modifications”; they also search across multiple evolutionary paths.

Multi-lineage search:

  • Darwin Gödel Machine(DGM): Maintains an archive of coding agent variants, selects parents from it, has the parents modify their own implementations, and then evaluates the offspring on executable coding tasks. Keeping multiple lineages has two benefits: a single harmful modification cannot permanently block progress, and temporarily weaker variants can still serve as useful stepping stones.
  • Huxley–Gödel Machine(HGM): Further points out that current benchmark performance is not the same as "the ability to produce better descendants." It uses the agent's lineage of descendants to estimate Metaproductivity, allocating evaluation resources to agents with stronger long-term improvement potential. Put simply: what matters is not your current score, but how strong your “descendants” can become.

Population methods expand what can be inherited across lineages:

  • Group-Evolving Agents:when producing a new population, multiple parents share successful modifications and lessons from failures, so useful discoveries don't get trapped in a single branch.
  • DarwinX: Evolves a population of complete harnesses around a frozen model. A variant is accepted only if it expands task coverage without regressing on cases that are already solved.
  • HELIX: Proposes a model–framework loop. Framework evolution both improves current execution and produces verified trajectories to update the model; once the model changes, the framework is rebuilt for it.

C.3.4 Toward Recursively Improving Software Engineering Agentsp.77–78

The authors summarize the software engineering RSI loop in five steps:Development challenge → agent-level modification → repository-based trials → regression-aware selection → versioned inheritance. Repository feedback triggers a persistent agent update. The update is first evaluated on the current task and on held-out tasks, then inherited by future development and self-improvement as a traceable, reversible version.

Current status by level:

  • B0: within-task correction is common.
  • L1: Cross-task experience and skill learning, e.g., SWE-Exp and CODESKILL.
  • L2: The most solid level. Selects modifications to frameworks, tools, and workflows under a fixed objective and evaluator. Examples include Self-Harness, Agentic Harness Engineering, and DarwinX.
  • L3: Emerging, in the form of task generation based on learner state. Examples include Self-play SWE-RL and Socratic-SWE.
  • L4: Environment adaptation is largely absent.
  • L5:SICA and DGM modify code that affects subsequent self-improvement, showing bounded L5 characteristics. HGM introduces lineage-based meta-selection but does not make the selection rule itself adaptive. Full L5 has not yet been achieved.

How far are we from true software engineering RSI? This requires robust specifications, attributable improvements, and reliable transfer across repositories and software versions. As test generation and improvement strategies become adaptive, user intent, safety constraints, auditability, and rollback must remain externally protected.

5. S4: RSI in Healthcarep.78–79

Scenario characteristics and three main challengesp.78

Healthcare covers tasks such as diagnosis, medical image analysis, and treatment planning. A typical loop is:

  1. Agents perform clinical tasks;
  2. Obtain verifiable feedback from diagnostic results, physician corrections, or clinical guidelines;
  3. Persistently update components such as clinical knowledge, reasoning strategies, and workflows;
  4. Improvements are stored in memory, skill libraries, or model parameters for reuse in later cases.

Three major challenges:

  • Unrestricted trial and error is not allowed: Clinical actions can harm patients and require expert, ethical, and institutional oversight.
  • Clinical feedback is delayed, heterogeneous, and confounded:it is hard to attribute patient outcomes to any single decision or update.
  • Improvement depends on people and institutions: An update verified in one place cannot be safely inherited unless its source, scope, and uncertainty are recorded.

Medical RSI is therefore a threefold problem: acquiring feedback safely, assigning credit reliably, and inheriting capabilities under governance. Evolvable components fall into three categories: clinical memory and knowledge, clinical reasoning strategies, and medical tools and workflows.

C.4.1 Evolving Clinical Memory and Knowledgep.78

This is the most common form of RSI in medicine: turning feedback from completed cases into persistent knowledge that shapes decisions for later patients.

Early systems: retain case-level experience.

  • MedAgent-Zero (in Agent Hospital):stores successful treatment cases and reflects on failed ones to distill reusable diagnostic rules. This way, even a doctor agent with frozen model weights can retrieve this experience in later simulated consultations.
  • Agent Mental Clinic: A supervisor module compares the psychiatric agent's diagnoses with the annotated results and writes the resulting diagnostic experience into hierarchical memory.
  • MedAgentSim: combines medical records with experience records so that diagnostic agents can reuse knowledge accumulated from earlier simulated doctor-patient interactions.

What these systems have in common is that the output of one consultation becomes part of the persistent context for future inference.

Recent methods: evolving the structure and reliability of memory, not just accumulating raw trajectories.

  • DxEvolve: distills the diagnostic process into "diagnostic cognitive primitives" that encode reusable patterns of information gathering and diagnostic reasoning and can be retrieved for new cases.
  • GSEM: organizes experience into a two-layer graph and uses subsequent feedback to recalibrate node quality and the edge weights between experiences. This changes both "what is remembered" and "which memories are considered applicable."
  • Evo-MedAgent: Maintains three complementary stores: retrospective clinical episodes, procedural heuristics, and tool reliability. After each chest X-ray case, these stores are updated through reflection, so later cases inherit both clinical lessons and revised levels of trust in the diagnostic tools.

Limitations: Most of the evidence comes from simulated or retrospective cases with externally provided labels, and work validating the effect of evolution on real cases is still lacking.

C.4.2 Evolving Clinical Reasoning Strategiesp.78–79

Beyond clinical facts, agents can also evolve their strategies for "how to obtain, interpret and integrate evidence."

  • EvoClinician: Uses a Diagnose–Grade–Evolve loop. The Actor asks questions and orders tests step by step; the Process Grader scores each action by clinical benefit and resource cost; the Evolver uses the feedback to revise the Actor's prompts and memory before the next case. What gets inherited is a diagnostic strategy, such as which symptom to clarify first and which test to prioritize.
  • EvoMDT: Evolves reasoning at the level of multi-agent coordination. It splits oncology decisions across agents playing different specialist roles and uses expert evaluation and outcome-based signals to update prompts, consensus weights, and retrieval scope. As a result, feedback changes not only the reasoning of individual agents but also how specialist opinions are weighted and reconciled in later cases.
  • PsychAgent: For psychological counseling, it extracts practice-based skills from past counseling trajectories, adds them to a continually evolving skill library, and then internalizes selected skills into the model through rejection fine-tuning.

C.4.3 Evolving Medical Tools and Workflowsp.79

The third route turns successful executions into reusable tools, skills, or clinical workflows, evolving the agent's action library.

At the action-space level:

  • MACRO:Starting from a fixed set of medical imaging tools, it identifies recurring multi-step patterns in verified execution traces, synthesizes them into composite tools, and registers them as new high-level actions. So it changes “what can be called directly,” not just retrieval of past solutions.
  • SkeMex: Does something similar at the level of procedural skills. It distills informative clinical trajectories into three tiers of skills (general, task-specific, and action-level), estimates each skill's utility in different contexts from environment feedback, and promotes, merges, updates, or deletes entries through a Read–Write–Assess–Govern lifecycle.

In both, inheritance happens through an evolving action space whose components are selected by their demonstrated utility on later tasks.

Larger analysis workflows:

  • TissueLab: lets domain experts inspect intermediate results on medical images and provide corrections, which guide active learning, classifier improvement, and subsequent workflow construction, covering tasks in pathology, radiology, spatial omics, and more.
  • HealthFlow: turns completed electronic health record (EHR) analyses into persistent safety guardrails, reusable workflows, dataset-specific anchors, and code snippets. An evaluator first identifies execution or methodological flaws, then a reflector validates, updates, or retires the experiences before they are made available for retrieval in later analyses.

Section summary: These systems move medical RSI from “selecting predefined tools” to “building reusable workflows.” But technical success, or physician feedback on individual cases, does not guarantee safety across the whole patient population. Evolved workflows must therefore be validated and reversible before they are inherited across cases.

C.4.4 Toward Recursively Improving Medical Agentsp.79

The ultimate form of healthcare RSI is a system that keeps getting better at medical tasks while maintaining clinical effectiveness and safety. Each case should produce two things: the task result, and a verified improvement to memory, reasoning strategies, or the action library.

Current status by level:

  • B0: In-case reflection and answer revision are common, but they don't persistently change future behavior.
  • L1: Achieves persistent inheritance through case memory, diagnostic rules, and reusable clinical knowledge, e.g., Agent Mental Clinic, MedAgentSim, and DxEvolve.
  • L2: The strongest frontier. Systems can diagnose failures and autonomously select or apply updates to prompts, memory, reasoning strategies, tools, workflows, and coordination mechanisms, but objectives, evaluators, and deployment gates remain fixed externally. Examples include EvoClinician, MACRO, HealthFlow, GSEM, Evo-MedAgent, EvoMDT, and SkeMex.
  • L3-like:a few systems use current uncertainty or accumulated experience to shape subsequent evidence gathering, simulated consultations, or expert annotation, e.g., Agent Hospital and TissueLab. However, patient populations, task distributions, and acceptance criteria are still largely set externally.
  • L4-style:EvoPatient co-evolves simulated patients and simulated doctors, but the simulator's clinical realism and the evaluation criteria are still set externally.
  • True L4 and L5:end-to-end L4 adaptation based on real longitudinal outcomes, and L5 evolution of the clinical improver itself, have barely been attempted.

How far are we from true medical RSI? This requires three things: reliably attributing longitudinal patient outcomes, safely obtaining feedback without unrestricted clinical exploration, and selectively transferring updates across populations and institutions. Equally important is a governed "capability commitment" process: every persistent update must undergo clinical validation and prospective monitoring, be traceable to supporting evidence, and be revocable when its assumptions or safety guarantees no longer hold.
