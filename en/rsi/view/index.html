My take · RSI from an SA's perspective

After reviewing RSI Landscape, Investment perspective, AlphaEvolve Explained and those papers, the author's own conclusions.Everything on this page is judgment, not fact.Each one covers why it happens, the evidence, what it means for SAs, and when I'd be wrong. Compiled on 2026-11-18, to be scored again in 2027-05.

Core thesis:What RSI really changes isn't “how smart AI is,” but the cost structure of “improvement” — improvement used to be limited by scarce experts; now it's limited by Evaluator quality and Eval compute. So value will flow from models to evaluators, compute demand will shift from “big training peaks” to “a high-frequency long tail of evaluation,” and competition will come down to Who hosts the evals, and who controls the changes.

Thesis 1: Value is shifting from the “model” to the “evaluator”

  • Mechanism: In a “frozen model, automatically modify code / harness” loop, two of the three components are already swappable. The generation model can be swapped (Gemini, Claude, open source), and the evolution engine is open source (OpenEvolve and ShinkaEvolve share AlphaEvolve's structure).The only thing that can't be replaced is the customer's own evaluators, holdout sets, and historical trajectories, because they encode “what this company considers better.”
  • Evidence: AlphaEvolve's official design is “generation on Google Cloud, evaluation on the customer side,” and it states explicitly that it “does not run your code.” BASF's key work was first using three years of historical data to define the score for “how close the simulation is to reality.” JetBrains says “engineers still own the benchmarks, reviews, and release decisions.”
  • What it means for SAs: Customer stickiness isn't in “whose model does the generation,” but in Where evals run, and where trajectories and holdout sets are stored. Helping customers build evaluators, datasets and traces in their own accounts lasts longer than pushing any particular model. Put the other way: whoever owns the evaluator owns the loop.
  • I'm wrong if: If managed services start offering “industry-standard eval suites” and get customers to depend on them (the evaluator itself becomes part of the platform), stickiness shifts back to the platform.

Thesis 2: The shape of compute demand is changing — from “training peaks” to the “evaluation long tail”

  • Mechanism: Every round of evolution has to run each of dozens to hundreds of candidates. These jobs Small, short, highly parallel, interruptible, and each one isolated, and many are CPU tasks (compiling, simulation, running tests), not just GPU. Cold-start time, per-second billing, Spot availability, and strength of isolation will matter more than “how many GPUs you have.”
  • Evidence: In this demo, 97.7% of the cost of one round went to evaluation; Google built a microVM service specifically for agents (Agent Compute Engine, pitched as “eval sweeps don't wait on infrastructure”) and connected AlphaEvolve to Cloud Batch to fan out hundreds of evaluations; sandboxes have become a standalone business (Modal's sandbox business is reportedly at about $300M annualized).
  • What it means for SAs: For RSI-type workloads, the architecture review question isn't “how big is the training cluster” but “how do you schedule ten thousand ten-second isolated tasks, at what cost, and how fast do they start.” Cloud providers' existing fine-grained primitives (lightweight VMs, Batch, Spot, serverless) will be a hidden competitive advantage.
  • I'm wrong if: Evaluation is increasingly done by “learned verifier models” (scoring on GPUs instead of actually running the code), so demand shifts back toward GPU inference.

Thesis 3: Goodhart's law is the law of physics in this field — evaluation costs rise along with optimization capability

  • Mechanism: The stronger the optimizer, the more holes it finds in the evaluation (“when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”). So evaluation isn't a one-time investment but a Arms race: Each time optimization capability moves up a step, evaluations need more hidden test cases, rotated problems, and added constraints. Evaluation budgets should rise with optimization strength rather than stay fixed.
  • Evidence: In Google's video tutorial, the evolutionary loop “skips rendering to go faster,” and the only fix is adding an image-quality threshold. Weco measured about 63% of candidates gaming the metric. NVIDIA SoL-Pi hides the “capability floor” from the party being optimized. ByteDance's HarnessDev found that the agent's own judgments barely predict the next round's improvement.
  • A corollary: Progress will concentrate in The eval itself is hard to game domains — math (formal proofs), chips (equivalence checking), and kernels (timing on real hardware). This also explains why AlphaEvolve's strongest results are all in these areas.
  • What it means for SAs: You can offer customers a new service:“Red-teaming your evaluator”: Hunt specifically for holes in evaluations, the way security teams hunt for system vulnerabilities. Evaluation operations (EvalOps) will become ongoing work, like SecOps.
  • I'm wrong if: A general, reliable, cheap automatic verification method emerges (one that prevents score gaming without human-designed evaluations), and evaluation stops being the bottleneck. No signs of this so far.

Thesis 4: Adoption order is set by “the cost and speed of verification,” not by “where AI is smartest”

  • Mechanism: Whether a problem can be improved automatically depends on two things:Cost per evaluation, How soon we'll know. Ranked by these two factors, the order is roughly: kernels / compilers (seconds, exact) → math (formal) → scheduling, routing, production planning (simulators exist) → forecasting (needs backtesting, and must wait for real data) → product experience (weeks, noisy).
  • Evidence: Jeff Dean says “infinite acceleration isn't realistic” because experiments take physical time. AlphaEvolve's enterprise cases are almost all problems with simulation or backtesting (BASF supply chain twin, FM Logistic routing, Kinaxis forecasting, Coolblue demand forecasting).
  • A corollary:Simulators and digital twins are a prerequisite for RSI. BASF's first step wasn't optimization but building a digital twin that matches historical data. So RSI will drive demand for simulation and HPC — Google promoting AlphaEvolve at the SC26 supercomputing conference is a sign of this.
  • What it means for SAs: When choosing customers, first ask: “Do you have a simulator / backtesting / automated tests?” If not, the first project is helping them build the simulation or evaluation, not jumping straight to optimization. This is also the most natural entry point for HPC and data platforms.
  • I'm wrong if: Foundation models can reliably “predict experiment results,” replacing real simulation. Current papers show these predictions aren't yet reliable.

Thesis 5: Big vendors aren't selling “self-improvement”; they're selling “auditable automated changes”

  • Mechanism: What enterprises fear isn't changes that aren't good enough, but Regressions go unnoticed, and incidents can't be explained. That's why all three vendors package the loop as “human-in-the-loop”: offline evaluation, A/B tests, PRs for human review, regression-set gates, canary rollouts. The real product is Change governance — versions, provenance, who approved it, how to roll back.
  • Evidence: Microsoft's reference approach is “production traces → human labeling → optimization → human PR review → held-out regression set → canary rollout”; AWS AgentCore uses “offline batch evaluation + online A/B, ship only if it wins”; in the words of an AlphaEvolve customer, “engineers own the release decision.”
  • What it means for SAs: Don't pitch it as a new AI feature. Pitch it as“CI/CD for agents and algorithms”, plugged into the customer's existing code review, approval, and change management processes. Whoever integrates best with the customer's change process wins. That matters more than a few percentage points of optimization gains.
  • I'm wrong if: Enterprises start accepting “automatic deployment without human approval” (for example, allowing it first in low-risk scenarios). At that point, competition returns to “whose optimization results are better.”

Thesis 6: RSI's first commercial wins are “compute savings,” and that will grow usage, not shrink it

  • Mechanism: Almost all of the earliest results are about efficiency: Borg reclaiming 0.7% of compute, Gemini training 1% faster, FlashAttention 32.5% faster, SoL-Pi using about 45% fewer tokens, HarnessMM using 30%–88% fewer. Each task gets cheaper, but historically efficiency gains tend to drive more usage (the Jevons paradox): cheaper inference gets used in more places.
  • What it means for SAs: Once customers adopt these tools, bills for some workloads will drop. Proactively helping customers do this beats waiting for a competitor to do it. The money saved and the trust built usually turn into new workloads.
  • I'm wrong if: Demand is already saturated, and the saved compute has no new uses. Right now AWS says its capacity is largely sold out through 2027, and Google and Microsoft haven't cut capex either, so there's no sign of saturation.

Thesis 7: “Model neutrality” is a structural advantage, but others are catching up

  • Mechanism: Frontier labs' terms prohibit customers from using their services to train competing models. a16z's rationale for investing in Mirendil is that “structurally, there has to be an independent company.” The same logic applies to cloud:Platforms not tied to their own models are a natural fit for third parties to run RSI loops on.
  • Evidence and counterevidence: Recursive runs its research loop on AWS. But in November, Google Cloud said it is the only major cloud offering all three major closed-source models, so the neutrality card is being matched. The hosted version of AlphaEvolve still uses only Gemini.
  • What it means for SAs: Neutrality needs to be concrete: with the same evaluation suite and the same set of tasks, switching models for comparison means changing one parameter. Evaluations and trajectories stay in the customer's account, not locked to any one model vendor. Just saying “we have lots of models” is no longer enough.
  • I'm wrong if: Managed AlphaEvolve opens up to third-party models, or customers find there is only one “best generation model,” so neutrality doesn't matter.

Thesis 8: The last thing humans hand over is “evaluation authority” — and it's the skill SAs most need to practice

  • Mechanism: The AI4AI survey splits handing control to AI into three steps: first hand over Search direction, then hand over Search space, and only at the end hand over Eval signal(L3). Today's products all stop at the point where control over evaluation still rests with humans. In other words, over the next few years, human leverage will concentrate on “defining what good looks like.”
  • What it means for SAs: People who can write evaluations will be worth more than people who can write prompts. The most useful skill transfer an SA can give a customer is teaching teams to design evaluations: how to pick metrics, build held-out sets, prevent score gaming, and account for evaluation cost. This lasts longer than pitching any single product.
  • I'm wrong if: L3 (AI setting its own evaluations) lands reliably in enterprise settings. That would be true RSI, and most of the judgments on this page would need rewriting.

Thesis 9: The capital structure is “clouds writing call options on RSI demand”; SAs need to understand this time window

  • Mechanism: A large share of the money new labs raise becomes long-term compute contracts with a single cloud within a year (about 63% for Recursive, about half of Mirendil's seed round). By signing these contracts, cloud providers are betting that “RSI research will keep consuming lots of compute.” If the research goes well, demand keeps growing; if not, these companies will pivot or be acquired, and the contracts will be renegotiated.
  • What it means for SAs: New labs usually pick a compute partner within 1–3 months of raising, and once their evaluation and data pipelines are built on a cloud, they rarely move.The window is short, and it only opens once.
  • I'm wrong if: Labs widely adopt multi-cloud, portable architectures, or compute becomes a pure commodity that can be swapped at any time. Current large deals are all multi-year and single-cloud.

Where I'm least confident

  • How fast are things really moving inside frontier labs: The most important evidence (the labs' internal speedup data) is mostly redacted or unpublished. If labs are already close to L3 internally, this page's call that “enterprises stall at L2” will quickly become outdated.
  • Will models "learn" the eval?: If reliable learned verifiers emerge, judgments 1, 2, and 3 all need revising.
  • China Regions: There is little public information on the compute and technical approaches of China-based RSI companies and big tech firms, so this assessment relies mostly on inference.

Feel free to point out any judgment you think is wrong, or offer counterexamples.
